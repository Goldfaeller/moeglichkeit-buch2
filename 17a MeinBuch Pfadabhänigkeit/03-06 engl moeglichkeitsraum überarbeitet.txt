== 3.6 Der Unterschied zur Biologie: Absicht ==

:1. On April 26, 1956, a converted oil tanker named the Ideal X left Port Newark for Houston with fifty-eight removable freight containers secured above her deck.
:2. The man behind the voyage, Malcolm McLean, had built a large trucking business before acquiring a steamship company in 1955.
:3. A familiar story places his decisive insight years earlier on a dock, where he watched workers transfer cargo piece by piece between a truck and a ship.
:4. The story has been repeated more confidently than its details can be documented, but the operational question it captures was real: why handle the goods again when the freight box itself could move?
:5. Load the cargo inland, lift the box from truck to ship, and send the same sealed unit onward at the other end.
:6. That sequence sounds obvious only because a vast transport system now makes it routine.
:7. McLean did not invent the container as an object.
:8. Railways had used transferable freight boxes for decades, European operators had developed their own systems, and the United States military moved supplies in steel CONEX boxes.
:9. Matson Navigation Company was also developing a different container service for trade between the American West Coast and Hawaii.
:10. What McLean and his collaborators demonstrated was the power of treating truck, crane, ship, terminal, and box as one continuous system.
:11. The box mattered because the cargo no longer had to be unpacked whenever the mode of transport changed.
:12. That reduced handling, shortened time in port, and made damage and theft easier to control.
:13. The visible invention was rectangular steel, but the consequential invention was coordination.
:14. Even that achievement had no single author.
:15. McLean supplied capital, commercial pressure, and an integrating idea, while engineers such as Keith Tantlinger developed crucial handling and securing equipment.
:16. Port authorities, shipyards, trucking firms, railways, manufacturers, standards bodies, regulators, and customers then had to make the system interoperable.
:17. This is what intention looks like in technology.
:18. We build models, run simulations, write specifications, argue in committees, and change our minds about a design before a single component is manufactured.
:19. Evolution has no vantage point outside the organism, no drawing board, and no meeting at which the participants agree to pursue a distant goal.
:20. If foresight were enough to escape the weight of the past, technology would have escaped it long ago.
:21. McLean's early containers reflected the world in which his trucks already operated.
:22. His boxes grew from thirty-three to thirty-five feet in length, dimensions shaped in part by highway limits in the regions his company served.
:23. Matson favored twenty-four-foot boxes for its own ships and cargo, while other operators chose still other dimensions and fittings.
:24. Each design could work inside its own company and still fail as part of a common network.
:25. A crane built for one corner fitting could not safely lift every rival box, and a chassis spaced for one length could not automatically carry another.
:26. Incompatibility therefore imposed a choice on ports and shippers before containerization had proved its full value.
:27. Committing to one proprietary system meant buying specialized equipment and risking isolation if another system prevailed.
:28. Waiting for agreement meant postponing the savings that had made the idea attractive.
:29. Deliberation did not remove the coordination problem; it made the problem visible enough to negotiate.
:30. American standards work accelerated in the late 1950s, and the International Organization for Standardization created its freight-container technical committee in 1961.
:31. The committees did more than select a convenient length.
:32. They reconciled highway practice, railway impacts, ship motion, lifting methods, stacking loads, identification rules, and the strength of floors, walls, roofs, doors, and corner fittings.
:33. Tests and negotiations altered McLean's design in almost every important technical detail.
:34. His companies made relevant patents available to the standardization effort without royalties, allowing other firms to build compatible equipment.
:35. The emerging system centered on lengths that included twenty and forty feet, an eight-foot width, standardized corner fittings, and common requirements for handling and strength.
:36. The result was not the clean execution of one inventor's blueprint but a negotiated architecture that no participant could have imposed alone.
:37. Its compromises did not make every container identical, because forty-five-foot units, high-cube boxes, tanks, platforms, and domestic variants also found places in the larger family.
:38. Standardization narrowed variation where interchange required agreement and left variation where the network could tolerate it.
:39. That distinction explains both the usefulness and the binding force of the system.
:40. A shared geometry lets a box move among ships, trains, trucks, cranes, depots, and software without being repacked at every boundary.
:41. Each compatible investment increases the value of the investments already made.
:42. More standardized boxes justify more specialized terminals, and more specialized terminals make standardized boxes more attractive.
:43. Operators learn to load them faster, manufacturers improve the equipment, insurers understand the risks, and lenders become more willing to finance the network.
:44. These are positive feedbacks in the causal sense, not a verdict that every consequence is good.
:45. The same feedbacks that create enormous gains also raise the cost of changing the agreed dimensions or fittings.
:46. A new box must fit not only inside a ship but also inside a web of calculations, contracts, safety rules, maintenance routines, and trained movements.
:47. The metal is the least of it, because changing the box alone does not change the network.
:48. This is lock-in, but it is not immobility.
:49. The coexistence of several container lengths shows that a standard can admit controlled variation, and the standards themselves have been revised repeatedly.
:50. Its capacity for further change—its evolvability—can survive when adapters, versioning, dual compatibility, and transition funding keep a deeper change reachable.
:51. The Panama Canal offers the same principle at a larger scale.
:52. Its original lock chambers, completed before the canal opened in 1914, are 110 feet wide and 1,000 feet long.
:53. Those concrete dimensions helped define the maximum class of vessels known as Panamax and influenced ship design for a century.
:54. Yet Panama did not remain permanently trapped by them.
:55. New locks inaugurated in 2016 use chambers 55 meters wide and 427 meters long, opening the route to a larger class of vessels at the price of an immense construction project.
:56. A path can be changed, but the built environment determines the scale at which change becomes worthwhile.
:57. Intention also changes the size of the steps that technical development can take.
:58. An engineering team can build an unprofitable prototype, tolerate a temporary loss, or coordinate several complementary changes before any one of them pays.
:59. A biological population cannot suspend reproduction while its descendants wait for a master plan to become functional.
:60. It would still be wrong to conclude that evolution can never cross a valley of lower fitness.
:61. Genetic drift in small populations, recombination, multiple mutations, migration, and a changing environment can all make such crossings possible under particular conditions.
:62. The sharper difference is that engineers can represent a distant objective and deliberately finance intermediate states because of that representation.
:63. Technology also inherits sideways, and it can do so with unusual speed.
:64. A machine may combine a bearing from clockmaking, a material developed for aircraft, a control method from another industry, and software written on another continent.
:65. Biology is not confined to a perfectly branching family tree, because horizontal gene transfer is important in microbial evolution and hybridization can move genes between some lineages.
:66. Technical borrowing is nevertheless guided by documents, markets, institutions, and explicit search in ways that genetic transfer is not.
:67. What one generation of engineers learns can be written down, taught, and built into the next product within a working lifetime.
:68. That does not mean that every abandoned technology can simply be resurrected from a patent.
:69. Drawings may survive while tacit skills, suppliers, materials, tools, and reasons for old choices disappear.
:70. Technical inheritance is therefore more deliberate than genetic inheritance, but it is not frictionless or complete.
:71. Adoption is also not the technical equivalent of biological survival.
:72. A product may spread because it is cheap, compatible, subsidized, fashionable, legally required, or expected to win, while an excellent design may fail because its complements are missing.
:73. The evolutionary analogy reveals cumulative change, but it breaks if market success is mistaken for fitness or intention is mistaken for omniscience.
:74. Sweden's switch from left-hand to right-hand road traffic shows how far coordinated intention can reach.
:75. In a 1955 advisory referendum, 82.9 percent voted to retain left-hand traffic, while 15.5 percent supported a switch.
:76. The Swedish parliament nevertheless approved right-hand traffic in 1963 after cross-border traffic and the growing vehicle fleet strengthened the case for compatibility with neighboring countries.
:77. Four years of preparation followed.
:78. Road signs and markings were replaced or covered, junctions were altered, buses and stops required changes, officials trained, and a national information campaign repeated the new rule through nearly every available medium.
:79. At 4:50 on the morning of September 3, 1967, the vehicles still permitted on the roads stopped, moved carefully to the right, and waited until traffic resumed at five.
:80. The change worked because many linked elements moved together on a preannounced date.
:81. It also worked because public authority could set the rule for an entire road network and devote years of money and labor to the transition.
:82. The immediate decline in crashes should not be credited to the side change alone, because temporary speed restrictions and unusually cautious driving accompanied it.
:83. Nor does the Swedish case prove that every network can be switched by decree.
:84. A road rule has a decisive advantage over a container terminal or an aircraft fleet: much of its installed base can remain physically in place once signs, markings, vehicles, and behavior have been coordinated.
:85. Sweden is therefore a counterexample to fatalism, not a recipe for costless redesign.
:86. Revolutionary France provides a more complicated comparison because several deliberate reforms launched under related ideals met very different fates.
:87. French measurements before the Revolution formed a dense and inconsistent patchwork whose unit names and quantities varied across places and trades.
:88. Reformers sought units that could be reproduced, related by powers of ten, and presented as universal rather than local privileges.
:89. The meter was defined as one ten-millionth of a quarter of Earth's meridian, and the metric system received a legal foundation in 1795.
:90. Its rational structure did not produce instant adoption.
:91. Instruments had to be manufactured, merchants and officials had to learn the units, conversion created disputes, and familiar measures remained embedded in daily exchange.
:92. In 1812 the government permitted hybrid customary measures based on metric quantities, and exclusive compulsory use in France did not begin until 1840.
:93. Intention succeeded here through persistence, administration, education, scientific use, and widening international coordination rather than through logical elegance alone.
:94. The Republican calendar followed a different path.
:95. Adopted in 1793 and dated back to the founding of the Republic in September 1792, it divided the year into twelve thirty-day months followed by five or six complementary days.
:96. Each month contained three ten-day periods, while the months received new seasonal names.
:97. A related scheme divided the day into ten hours of one hundred minutes, with one hundred seconds to the minute.
:98. Decimal time became obligatory only for a short interval from 1794 until its suspension in April 1795, although clocks displaying it continued to exist.
:99. The Republican calendar lasted in official use until the end of 1805.
:100. These reforms were neither drafted by precisely the same people nor enacted on the same afternoon, and their outcomes cannot be reduced to one variable.
:101. The calendar collided with religious practice, work rhythms, existing clocks, international coordination, and changes in political legitimacy.
:102. Metric units also faced resistance, but their advantages could accumulate across science, taxation, engineering, commerce, and administration while conversion proceeded unevenly.
:103. The comparison refines the mechanism: entanglement raises switching costs, but institutions, benefits, and transition strategies determine whether those costs remain prohibitive.
:104. Language makes the same point without yielding a simple contest between good and bad design.
:105. In 1887, the Warsaw ophthalmologist L. L. Zamenhof published the first book describing the constructed language that became known as Esperanto.
:106. He deliberately reduced irregularity and designed a productive grammar to ease learning and international communication.
:107. Esperanto developed a durable global community and even native speakers, yet it did not become the world's common auxiliary language.
:108. That outcome does not demonstrate a technical defect in its grammar.
:109. A language becomes more useful as more people can speak, teach, publish, work, and raise children in it, so adoption depends on a self-reinforcing social network.
:110. The revival of spoken Hebrew during roughly the same era followed a very different institutional path.
:111. Hebrew had continued in religious, literary, and scholarly use, so the revival did not create a language from nothing.
:112. Families, teachers, schools, writers, language committees, political movements, and later public institutions expanded the settings in which it could serve everyday life.
:113. The contrast with Esperanto cannot be explained by linguistic merit alone, nor can it be separated from identity, migration, education, and state formation.
:114. Design supplies the form; coordinated practice supplies much of its future.
:115. Planning on apparently open ground creates another version of the same problem.
:116. Brasília was built as Brazil's new capital from 1956 to 1960 around Lúcio Costa's 1957 pilot plan.
:117. Its intersecting axes, hierarchical road network, specialized sectors, monumental government spaces, and residential superblocks embodied explicit ideas about circulation and urban life.
:118. Those choices delivered coherence, legibility, and an administrative center at extraordinary speed.
:119. They also made later changes begin from a strongly organized pattern rather than an empty plateau.
:120. Population growth did not leave Brasília frozen inside the plan, because development spread through satellite cities, peripheral settlements, and a broader metropolitan structure.
:121. Adaptation occurred partly by expanding beyond the most protected and tightly specified core.
:122. The case does not prove that planned cities are less adaptable than older cities in general.
:123. It shows instead that a comprehensive plan concentrates many decisions at the beginning, so later revisions must contend with several linked commitments at once.
:124. Planning does not remove the past; it can manufacture a large amount of consequential past at once and hand it to the next generation in a single piece.
:125. An aircraft family reveals the same process inside a product that engineers continue to redesign deliberately.
:126. The original Boeing 737 received its type certificate in 1967 and sat relatively low to the ground, a useful feature for servicing at airports with limited ground equipment.
:127. When the larger-diameter, more efficient CFM56 engine was adapted for the 737-300, ground clearance became a design constraint inherited from the earlier airframe.
:128. Engineers mounted the engine farther forward and higher, reduced the fan diameter, moved accessories from the bottom toward the sides, and shaped the lower nacelle accordingly.
:129. The low stance had been an excellent decision for its decade, and the distinctive later inlet was not evidence that engineers had overlooked an obvious solution.
:130. It was a new solution made within a geometry that had once solved a different operational problem.
:131. The physical fact of limited ground clearance is not itself path dependence.
:132. The path-dependent element is the continued value of the airframe lineage, supported by accumulated engineering knowledge, production tooling, certification work, maintenance systems, spare parts, airport equipment, airline fleets, and pilot training.
:133. The Federal Aviation Administration has certified numerous 737 variations under the type certificate that began with the original model.
:134. Commonality can reduce transition costs and preserve hard-won operational experience, which makes it a productive form of continuity rather than mere inertia.
:135. The same continuity narrows some redesign choices and increases the attraction of another derivative.
:136. A clean-sheet aircraft remains physically possible, but it must earn enough advantage to justify a second web of design, testing, production, certification, training, and support.
:137. Safety rules participate in this history without being reducible to an obstacle, because the evidence and routines accumulated around a known design can be valuable even as derivative development creates new questions.
:138. Intentional regulation can therefore stabilize an old path for good reasons while still requiring regulators to examine when inherited assumptions no longer fit.
:139. The container, the traffic rule, the measurement system, the language, the city, and the aircraft do not obey one universal law.
:140. They differ in physical durability, number of users, concentration of authority, ease of parallel operation, visibility of benefits, and tolerance for temporary incompatibility.
:141. Those differences are not complications to be swept aside; they determine the strength and reversibility of each path.
:142. Three reasons nevertheless recur across the cases.
:143. The first is the horizon.
:144. Foresight in engineering is genuine but short, calibrated to the working life of a product, the term of a contract, the career of a manager, the patience of an investor, and the evidence available at the time.
:145. Lock-in does not respect the horizon of the person who caused it, so a choice optimized for fifteen years may remain embedded for a century.
:146. The second reason is that intention operates on the object while path dependence operates on the web around it.
:147. A designer may have authority over the artifact and almost none over the suppliers who make its components, the workers trained to install it, the regulations written around it, or the neighboring devices that expect the old form.
:148. The third mechanism has no close biological counterpart at the level of market coordination: intention introduces shared expectation.
:149. When users must choose between incompatible systems, their estimate of what others will adopt can matter almost as much as present performance.
:150. If enough buyers expect one system to prevail, suppliers and trainers invest in it, making the expectation increasingly accurate.
:151. This feedback can close a contest early, but it does not guarantee that tests, prices, institutions, or later innovations will never reverse the result.
:152. Foresight does not only help us escape the past.
:153. It can coordinate a jump that unguided variation would be unlikely to assemble, and it can close off part of the future by coordinating expectations around one path before alternatives have been fully explored.
:154. No single actor needs to intend the resulting lock-in.
:155. There is an author for the box, and an author for the crane, and an author for the lock chamber, and a committee for the standard.
:156. There is no single author for the network in which they reinforce one another, but unlike a biological lineage, that network can be represented, governed, and renegotiated, although no actor enjoys an uncontested vantage point and renegotiation often has distributional costs.
:157. In biological evolution, history persists through inherited structures, developmental processes, and ecological relationships that must keep functioning as they change.
:158. In technology, history also persists in agreements, trained hands, tooling, regulation, buildings, data, supply chains, and expectations.
:159. The more deliberately these elements are coordinated, the more useful the system may become, while the cost of an incompatible improvement can rise with that usefulness.
:160. McLean saw more of the cargo-handling system than most of his contemporaries and changed it, which was a powerful use of foresight.
:161. What he could not do, and what no single actor can reliably do, is foresee the entire system that a successful change will create.
:162. The dimensions of his own boxes did not become the final global dimensions; what endured was the intermodal principle after committees, competitors, public agencies, and engineers reshaped it into a shared standard.
:163. Intention, therefore, does not abolish path dependence.
:164. It relocates and transforms it: planning opens a route, collective adoption widens it, complementary investment paves it, and the resulting success raises the cost of turning away.
:165. The first task of intention is to choose a design; the harder one is to decide what “better” should mean before widespread adoption raises the cost of choosing again.
