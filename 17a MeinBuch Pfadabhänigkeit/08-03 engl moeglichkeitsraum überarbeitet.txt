== 8.3 Das Familienrecht als Bruchstelle ==

:1. Open the Civil Code that took effect across the German Empire in 1900, and the hierarchy of the household appears in the grammar of its rules.
:2. The husband was entitled to decide matters concerning married life, including the couple's residence, while the wife was assigned the management of the household.
:3. She could work outside it only within limits set by her marital duties, and with authorization from the guardianship court her husband could terminate a contract for her personal services if her activity impaired marital interests.
:4. The statutory property regime placed the administration and use of much of the wife's property in his hands.
:5. For legitimate children, parental authority belonged primarily to the father, whereas a child born outside marriage was legally deemed not to be related to its father.
:6. Together they described the family the law treated as normal: a male-headed economic unit, a wife whose domestic labor defined her place, and children whose status depended heavily on their parents' marriage.
:7. That design was contested before the code ever came into force, including by women in the organized women's movement who identified its unequal consequences.
:8. The legislators therefore did not simply preserve an uncontested custom from time immemorial.
:9. They selected among contemporary positions and fixed one selection in a national code.
:10. Once enacted, the model acquired supports that an opinion alone does not possess: court routines, legal training, property arrangements, administrative forms, and expectations about who could promise what for a family.
:11. A rule about marital authority could thus influence a woman's employment contract, the location of a household, the control of assets, and the bargaining position of each spouse.
:12. Those connections made the old model durable, but they did not make it immutable.
:13. Family law would become one of the places where Germany's division revealed how quickly a legal layer could move when a new state wanted a different social norm.
:14. After 1945, both emerging German states inherited this body of law, though occupation measures, wartime legislation, judicial practice, and radically different political orders meant that the starting conditions were not identical.
:15. Both also confronted households altered by death, displacement, destroyed housing, and women's wartime and postwar work.
:16. Nothing in that situation dictated a single legal response.
:17. In the West, the Basic Law of 1949 declared men and women equal, yet Article 117 temporarily preserved conflicting law until it could be adjusted, with a deadline of March 31, 1953.
:18. The deadline passed before Parliament completed the reform.
:19. The old hierarchy consequently became a constitutional problem as well as a social inheritance, and courts had to operate during the gap between a new principle and an unfinished body of ordinary law.
:20. West Germany's Equality Act was passed in 1957 and largely took effect on July 1, 1958.
:21. It abolished the husband's general final say and replaced his administration of marital property with a statutory regime in which each spouse kept separate ownership while gains accumulated during marriage were shared when that regime ended.
:22. Yet the revised law still assumed that the wife normally ran the household and permitted paid work only when compatible with her duties in marriage and family.
:23. It also left the father a deciding vote in disputes over children until the Federal Constitutional Court invalidated that preference.
:24. This sequence matters because it separates a declared principle from the machinery that gives the principle daily effect.
:25. A constitution can remove the legitimacy of an old hierarchy in one sentence, but thousands of family decisions still pass through statutes, judges, employers, assets, schools, tax rules, and habits.
:26. Legal change is therefore neither a magic switch nor mere symbolism.
:27. It alters enforceable rights and bargaining positions, while its practical reach depends on the institutions and material options surrounding those rights.
:28. West Germany did change course, but negotiation among parties, churches, jurists, ministries, and legislators made the transition slower and more internally compromised than the constitutional sentence suggested.
:29. The Soviet occupation zone and then the German Democratic Republic opened another.
:30. The East German Constitution of 1949 proclaimed equal rights for men and women, protected marriage and family, and called for public institutions that would help women combine motherhood with participation in social and economic life.
:31. The Law on the Protection of Mothers and Children and the Rights of Women, adopted on September 27, 1950, translated part of that program into ordinary law.
:32. It declared rules that diminished women's family rights abolished and replaced the husband's sole authority with joint decision-making by both spouses.
:33. It also addressed residence, household management, and child-rearing as matters for common decision rather than commands flowing from a household head.
:34. At the same time, it called for more childcare facilities, workplace opportunities, training, and public participation for women.
:35. The family-law break was therefore tied to a much larger project of building a socialist labor force and social order.
:36. War losses, postwar migration, and chronic labor shortages made women's work economically important, while paid employment was presented politically as the route to independence and equal citizenship.
:37. These motives reinforced one another: an egalitarian legal claim helped legitimize labor mobilization, and the demand for labor gave the claim institutional weight.
:38. Childcare was not an optional kindness attached to that design.
:39. If mothers were expected to remain in paid work, crèches, kindergartens, schools, health services, leave rules, and workplace arrangements became complementary institutions without which the new norm could not function.
:40. Each investment then made the dual-earner household more practicable and gave administrators, enterprises, and families reasons to plan around its continuation.
:41. This is the path-dependent mechanism in its concrete form.
:42. An early legal choice changed where the state invested, those investments changed which family arrangements were feasible, and the resulting routines increased the cost of returning to a system organized around a single male wage.
:43. The sequence was gradual rather than instantaneous.
:44. The 1950 statute removed central elements of marital hierarchy, but the German Democratic Republic did not obtain a self-contained Family Code until December 1965, with the code taking effect in 1966.
:45. During the interval, inherited provisions, constitutional commands, new statutes, and judicial interpretations had to be fitted together.
:46. That mixed period is evidence against the tempting story that a revolutionary government can write an old legal world out of existence overnight.
:47. It also shows why family law was more pliable than the whole architecture of private law.
:48. Changing who decided within a marriage required consequential revisions, but it did not demand a simultaneous replacement of every rule governing sales, land, inheritance, legal personality, and civil procedure.
:49. The state could detach this layer, supply new guiding principles, and build specialized institutions around it while much of the older legal vocabulary remained available elsewhere.
:50. The Family Code sharpened the new model by describing equality as decisive for socialist family life and by assigning both spouses a share in childcare and household work.
:51. It joined personal relations to public purposes with unusual candor, presenting the development of each spouse's abilities as valuable both to the individual and to society.
:52. This language widened women's lawful room to study, work, hold property, and participate beyond the home, but it did not create an unrestricted private sphere.
:53. The same state that rejected the husband's authority claimed broad authority to define responsible marriage, desirable child-rearing, and useful participation in collective life.
:54. Early East German reform was therefore not simply a faster version of liberal individual emancipation in the West.
:55. It substituted a different institutional normality, one centered on the employed mother, public provision, and an explicitly social function for the family.
:56. That normality opened possibilities that the older breadwinner model had narrowed, especially economic independence and continuous employment for women.
:57. It also made other choices harder to sustain when benefits, workplace expectations, and public approval were organized around full participation in paid labor.
:58. Formal equality, moreover, could assign a duty to both spouses without dividing the hours between them.
:59. Public childcare could remove part of the day's care burden without cooking dinner, washing clothes, nursing a sick child at night, or changing what colleagues expected of mothers and fathers.
:60. The law had redrawn the family's boundary of authority, but the distribution of ordinary labor inside that boundary remained a more stubborn question.
:61. In East German households, that stubbornness appeared as the much-discussed double burden: women entered paid employment in very large numbers without men assuming an equal share of unpaid work.
:62. The phrase can sound like a general complaint unless its two mechanisms are kept distinct, because adding paid work and redistributing unpaid work are not the same institutional operation.
:63. Employment policy increased the hours women owed to enterprises, while durable gender expectations continued to assign them primary responsibility for children and the home.
:64. The Family Code challenged that assignment in principle, but a sentence imposing shared responsibility could not monitor every meal, errand, illness, or interrupted night.
:65. Nor did a state committed to production necessarily value unpaid domestic work as highly as labor counted in an economic plan, even though every workplace depended on care performed elsewhere.
:66. Equality at the factory gate could therefore coexist with inequality after the shift ended.
:67. The contradiction was not proof that the legal reform meant nothing.
:68. It was proof that several paths can occupy the same household at once.
:69. The reform had changed who could lawfully decide, but not the time, services, or bargaining power with which spouses carried those decisions out.
:70. Public childcare changed the feasible choices in a direct and substantial way.
:71. A mother with a reliable place for her child could maintain employment, preserve skills, accumulate seniority, and receive her own income in ways that a formal right to work alone could not secure across successive years.
:72. Those accumulated advantages mattered across a working life, even when wages, occupations, promotion, and access to positions of authority remained unequal.
:73. Yet institutional care covered specified places and times, whereas family responsibility spread across evenings, shortages, medical appointments, school problems, and emotional attention.
:74. Policies intended to reconcile motherhood and employment were also commonly directed at mothers rather than fathers, embedding an unequal answer in measures designed to relieve inequality.
:75. Leave entitlements and workplace accommodations could ease women's immediate load while confirming that employers should treat women, not men, as the workers likely to interrupt employment for children.
:76. A support could thus carry an old role inside a new social model.
:77. Path dependence often works through precisely such hybrids, in which reform removes one barrier while borrowing assumptions from the arrangement it seeks to replace.
:78. The comparison also resists a simple verdict about which legal system was more modern.
:79. East German women gained earlier access to a dense infrastructure for combining employment and motherhood, and the dual-earner family became ordinary rather than exceptional.
:80. They nevertheless lived under a dictatorship that restricted political choice, directed careers and education, constrained exit and dissent, and claimed the family as an instrument of socialist formation.
:81. West German spouses retained wider civil and political liberties, but family, tax, and social policy long supported a breadwinner-and-homemaker division that made continuous employment less accessible and less rewarding for many mothers.
:82. These were not two scores on a single scale.
:83. They were bundles of rights, services, expectations, and constraints whose components did not move together.
:84. Calling the division a natural experiment is therefore useful only if the word natural does not smuggle in the controls of a laboratory.
:85. The populations experienced different economies, migration streams, political institutions, international alignments, religious environments, and degrees of coercion, any of which could shape family behavior independently of a family code.
:86. That qualification sharpens rather than destroys the legal comparison.
:87. Rules mattered because they authorized decisions, allocated property and responsibility, and signaled which arrangements public institutions would support.
:88. They mattered most when connected to employment, childcare, education, housing, and social benefits, because those neighboring systems determined whether a formal option was practically usable.
:89. Where those complements were absent, a declaration could outrun daily possibility, leaving a person legally free to choose what income, time, or available services made unreachable.
:90. Where they were present, law helped create routines that survived changes in personal conviction and, in some respects, even the state that built them.
:91. The observed difference cannot be credited to statutes alone, but neither can statutes be removed from the chain of causes.
:92. They were joints linking a social ideal to budgets, organizations, and household bargaining.
:93. That is more consequential than symbolism and less omnipotent than command.
:94. Family law was relatively plastic because many of its guiding rules could be detached from the technical core of property transfer and contract without making ordinary exchange impossible.
:95. A new state could abolish a husband's final authority on Monday without requiring every shop, registry, and court to learn a new concept of ownership by Tuesday.
:96. Constitutional principles also supplied a powerful language for judges to disapply or reinterpret inherited provisions before a complete code was ready.
:97. The smaller number of specialized interfaces reduced the coordination cost of change, although registries, courts, welfare offices, and families still had to absorb the new rules.
:98. This relative plasticity helps explain why the East could remove patriarchal rules years before replacing the BGB as a whole and why the West could constitutionalize equality before fully reconstructing marital law.
:99. It does not mean that family law was culturally shallow.
:100. On the contrary, rules about marriage, parenthood, names, legitimacy, property, and separation touch identities, religious commitments, economic security, and the care of children.
:101. Technical detachment can make legislation easier while moral conflict makes agreement harder, so political resistance and systemic replacement costs need not rise and fall together.
:102. The distinction between legal and cultural depth prevents two opposite errors.
:103. One error treats every survival from the old code as evidence of an unbreakable lock-in.
:104. Some provisions remained simply because legislators gave scarce time to more urgent disputes, courts had drained them of practical force, or few organized actors considered formal repeal worth spending political capital on a battle.
:105. The other error treats rapid enactment as rapid social transformation.
:106. A statute can alter the available moves immediately, yet people may still lack income, housing, childcare, cooperation, or a socially acceptable story with which to use them.
:107. Family law therefore shows a layered transition rather than a clean replacement: commands can disappear, entitlements can expand, supporting institutions can grow over decades, and inherited roles can continue inside the new arrangement under altered names.
:108. That pattern leaves room for agency because couples, judges, employers, and administrators do not respond identically.
:109. It also leaves room for feedback because their repeated responses determine which new rights become routine and which remain mostly on paper.
:110. Two legal problems reveal how differently those layers can bind.
:111. One was the old claim known as Kranzgeld, a payment that an “innocent” fiancée could demand under specified conditions after a broken engagement if she had had sexual relations with her fiancé.
:112. The provision encoded a world in which a woman's premarital chastity was treated as an economic asset diminished by sex and restored imperfectly by damages.
:113. It carried enormous symbolic freight but had few indispensable connections to the operation of the wider legal system, so removing it threatened neither transactions nor administrative coordination.
:114. As its social premise lost authority, judges and lawmakers could marginalize and eventually remove it without redesigning the surrounding structure of family life.
:115. Divorce law posed a different problem because the legal ground for ending a marriage affected evidence, courtroom strategy, maintenance, property, children, reputations, and negotiations long before a petition was ever filed.
:116. A system organized around proving marital fault encouraged spouses and lawyers to turn intimate failure into legally useful accusation, while a system organized around breakdown or the state's conception of a failed marriage generated different incentives.
:117. Changing that system required more than striking an archaic sentence, because consequences clustered around the gateway through which a marriage could end.
:118. The contrast is a warning against measuring the strength of a path by the emotional charge of its subject.
:119. A morally conspicuous rule may be legally isolated, while an apparently procedural rule quietly coordinates an entire field of choices.
:120. The family was a break point in Germany's divided legal history, but within that break some pieces proved brittle and others bent only when the connected structure moved with them.
