== 3.2 QWERTZ und die Macht der Gewöhnung ==

:1. Somewhere near you, probably within arm's reach, is a sheet of glass with letters painted on it.
:2. It has no moving parts, no levers, no springs, and nothing inside it can jam.
:3. The letters are arranged in an order that begins, along the top row, with Q, W, E, R, T, and then either Y or Z, depending on the language settings.
:4. That family of arrangements took shape in the 1870s around machines whose thin metal typebars swung toward paper.
:5. The arms are gone.
:6. The jamming problem is gone.
:7. The pivots, springs, ink ribbon, and most of the physical apparatus surrounding the early arrangement have disappeared as well.
:8. The arrangement stayed.
:9. It traveled through mechanical typewriters, electric typewriters, teleprinters, computer terminals, personal computers, laptops, phones, and touchscreens.
:10. The object under your fingers therefore presents a genuine historical puzzle, although not quite the tidy fable often told about it.
:11. The story begins with Christopher Latham Sholes, a Wisconsin printer, editor, and inventor who worked with Carlos Glidden, Samuel Soulé, and the promoter James Densmore on an early writing machine.
:12. A patent issued to Sholes, Glidden, and Soulé in 1868 shows a machine with a circular assembly of typebars and a keyboard unlike the modern one.
:13. Other surviving evidence indicates that an early keyboard placed letters in a nearly alphabetical order across piano-like rows.
:14. The inventors did not move from that starting point to QWERTY in one inspired leap.
:15. They altered the machine repeatedly while struggling with alignment, paper movement, printing visibility, and the coordination of keys with typebars.
:16. On an upstrike machine, the bars hit the underside of the paper carriage, so the operator could not watch each character appear.
:17. Typebars could also collide or stick near the printing point when the mechanism was driven too quickly or awkwardly.
:18. Those facts gave the designers reasons to care about which bars moved in close succession.
:19. They do not prove the popular claim that Sholes deliberately scrambled the alphabet to make typists slow down.
:20. A layout intended merely to suppress speed would have been a peculiar selling point for a machine advertised as faster than handwriting.
:21. More important, the physical arrangement of surviving early machines does not consistently separate all common English letter pairs.
:22. The familiar claim turns a messy design history into a single-purpose tale that the documentary record cannot support.
:23. Telegraphy offers a more substantial part of the explanation.
:24. One early machine went to Edward Payson Porter, who ran a telegraph college in Chicago, and later prototypes were demonstrated to people in the telegraph business.
:25. Historians Koichi and Motoko Yasuoka have argued from machines, patents, correspondence, and American Morse practice that telegraph receivers helped shape several changes in the keyboard.
:26. In American Morse, some signals could remain ambiguous until more characters arrived, which made the placement of particular letters practically important to an operator transcribing a message.
:27. Yet this account should not be promoted into a replacement legend with one clean cause.
:28. The Yasuokas themselves describe a winding sequence involving inventors, telegraph users, manufacturers, patent constraints, and changing typing methods.
:29. The precise contribution of each pressure remains debated because the evidence is incomplete and some intermediate layouts must be reconstructed.
:30. In March 1873, Densmore contracted with E. Remington and Sons to manufacture the substantially revised Sholes and Glidden machine.
:31. Remington put the first commercial model on the market in 1874, and its capital-letter keyboard was recognizably QWERTY but not yet identical to every later version.
:32. Further mechanical and keyboard changes followed, including the arrival of a shift mechanism on the Remington No. 2 in 1878.
:33. A charming detail is often added at this point: the letters in TYPEWRITER can all be found on the top row.
:34. The observation is true, but the claim that Remington arranged the row so salesmen could perform that trick lacks secure evidence.
:35. Such stories are appealing because they provide an author, an intention, and a date.
:36. What the record provides instead is a succession of revisions, each made within a machine and a market that were also changing.
:37. No participant needed to intend the arrangement to survive for a century and a half.
:38. Survival became more likely only after the arrangement escaped from the machine that first carried it.
:39. During the 1880s and 1890s, rival typewriters used different printing mechanisms and offered competing keyboards.
:40. Some designs avoided typebar collisions altogether, so the original mechanical problem did not dictate the future standard.
:41. If mechanical necessity had been the only force at work, QWERTY might have vanished with the mechanism that favored it.
:42. Instead, typing became part of a larger system of work.
:43. Touch typing emerged in the late 1880s, and schools, manuals, contests, and employers began to organize training around particular finger movements.
:44. A keyboard that had once been searched visually became one that trained operators could use without looking at the keys.
:45. That advance stored the layout in human skill.
:46. Training also created an asymmetry between people and machines.
:47. A manufacturer could change the letters printed on new keytops more cheaply than an experienced typist could rebuild a practiced sequence of movements.
:48. Employers therefore preferred machines that trained applicants already knew.
:49. Students preferred to learn the layout used by prospective employers.
:50. Schools found it cheaper to teach the layout demanded by both groups, and their graduates enlarged the pool of compatible labor.
:51. Each choice made the next similar choice a little safer and a competing choice a little riskier.
:52. This was positive feedback, not merely the passage of time.
:53. The growing installed base lowered the cost of finding trained operators, buying familiar machines, and providing standardized instruction.
:54. It also raised the expected cost of choosing a layout that fewer people and firms could use.
:55. By the mid-1890s, major producers were converging on the four-row arrangement marketed as the Universal keyboard.
:56. The name described an aspiration before it described a fact, but standardization helped make the aspiration true.
:57. Economist Paul David later called attention to three interacting forces in this history: compatibility among parts of the system, economies created by widespread use, and investments in learning that could not be recovered simply by switching.
:58. The combination is more informative than any claim about a deliberately bad design.
:59. It explains how an early lead could reshape the cost of later choices even after competing hardware became available.
:60. It also explains why the keyboard's history is not just a story about habit.
:61. A private habit can disappear when one person decides to change it.
:62. A standard is distributed across the expectations and investments of many people who must continue to work together.
:63. The distinction becomes visible in the national variants of the keyboard.
:64. German-language layouts commonly exchange Y and Z and provide direct access to umlauts and the letter ß, while French-language users commonly encounter AZERTY arrangements.
:65. These are not fresh designs built from first principles for each language.
:66. They are local revisions of an inherited family, adjusted where a language community had enough reason and coordination to move together.
:67. The boundary between QWERTY and QWERTZ is therefore less like a wall than a seam.
:68. Some changes crossed that seam because key manufacturers, teachers, publishers, employers, and users converged on them within a shared market.
:69. More radical changes had to compete not with an isolated keyboard but with an entire population of compatible hands and machines.
:70. Local adaptation remained possible while replacement of the underlying pattern became expensive.
:71. The best-known attempt at replacement began with August Dvorak and William Dealey, who filed a patent application for their English-language keyboard in 1932 and received the patent in 1936.
:72. Their design placed heavily used letters on the home row and sought to distribute work efficiently between the hands.
:73. It was a systematic response to measured letter frequencies and to ideas about the motions involved in skilled typing.
:74. On a diagram, it looks more deliberate than QWERTY because its organizing principles are easier to state.
:75. A clear principle, however, does not by itself establish a superior working system.
:76. Superiority might mean higher maximum speed, faster learning, fewer errors, less finger travel, greater comfort, lower strain, or easier compatibility with existing work.
:77. Those goals overlap, but they are not identical.
:78. A layout can reduce calculated finger travel without producing the same proportional gain in real transcription, composition, or editing.
:79. Skilled typing depends on patterns of movement, anticipation, language, error correction, and the cognitive task that produces the words.
:80. The keyboard is one constraint in that performance, not the entire explanation of it.
:81. Dvorak and his supporters reported striking improvements, and wartime work associated with the United States Navy became the most famous evidence for rapid retraining gains.
:82. Those claims later acquired the authority of experimental fact in popular accounts of QWERTY.
:83. The evidence is weaker than that reputation suggests.
:84. The Navy work was conducted under Dvorak's supervision, involved limited groups, and has been criticized for its controls, comparisons, and reporting.
:85. A 1956 study for the General Services Administration retrained ten experienced QWERTY typists on Dvorak while giving a comparison group further QWERTY training.
:86. Its results did not show Dvorak retraining outperforming continued training on the established layout during the study period.
:87. That small government experiment did not settle every question about lifetime performance or comfort either.
:88. It did show how much the answer depends on whom one trains, for how long, for which task, and against what comparison.
:89. In 1982, Donald Norman and Diane Fisher reported simulations in which Dvorak improved predicted expert performance by about five percent over QWERTY, far below the dramatic gains often advertised.
:90. A simulation of keying constraints is useful evidence about a mechanism, but it is not a century-long field experiment.
:91. Claims about comfort and repetitive strain are even harder to isolate because posture, keyboard geometry, workload, breaks, force, and individual physiology also matter.
:92. The responsible conclusion is not that Dvorak offers no benefits.
:93. It is that the size and kind of any advantage remain sensitive to the measure and the evidence.
:94. This uncertainty produced an unusually revealing argument in economics.
:95. David's 1985 account made QWERTY a central example of how increasing returns and learning can lock in a technical standard.
:96. In 1990, Stan Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis challenged both the historical narrative and the evidence that Dvorak was decisively better.
:97. Their criticism weakened the popular story in which an obviously inferior keyboard defeated an obvious superior.
:98. It did not make the installed skills, compatibility effects, and coordination costs disappear.
:99. The two questions must be kept separate.
:100. One asks whether QWERTY is worse than a specified alternative by a specified measure.
:101. The other asks whether earlier adoption changed the cost and probability of adopting alternatives later.
:102. Evidence for the second does not prove the first.
:103. Conversely, uncertainty about the first does not erase the second.
:104. A lock-in can be real even when the locked-in standard is adequate and the challenger is only marginally better, better for some users, or not better at all.
:105. Indeed, a useful common standard may be stable precisely because it provides value every day.
:106. Modern software makes the distinction sharper.
:107. On many devices, an individual can change the logical keyboard layout through a setting rather than replacing any machinery.
:108. The letters on physical keycaps may then disagree with the characters produced, but touch typists can operate without relying on those labels.
:109. This cheap remapping proves that QWERTY is not a physical constraint and that its lock-in is not absolute.
:110. People do switch to Dvorak, Colemak, and other layouts, and some become proficient on more than one.
:111. Their success is a counterexample to the claim that an acquired layout cannot be changed.
:112. It is not a counterexample to path dependence.
:113. A person considering a switch must absorb a period of slower work, more errors, deliberate practice, and possible interference between learned patterns.
:114. The person may also need to use shared computers, labeled shortcuts, workplace support systems, examinations, or colleagues' devices designed around the familiar arrangement.
:115. An organization faces a larger coordination problem because training, equipment, documentation, help desks, hiring, and temporary losses of output must move together.
:116. The cost arrives early and visibly.
:117. Any benefit arrives later, varies by task and user, and remains uncertain in size.
:118. That distribution can make staying reasonable even for someone who believes another layout is somewhat better.
:119. It also prevents the simple inference that continued use reveals technical perfection.
:120. Market persistence shows that users value the whole compatible system more than the uncertain gain from changing one component at current prices.
:121. It does not tell us how the two systems would have compared if generations had learned them under otherwise similar conditions.
:122. There is no parallel world in which Dvorak, Colemak, or an unknown layout was taught in every school and installed in every office for a century.
:123. One historical trajectory is not a controlled comparison.
:124. This is why the most careful lesson from the keyboard is narrower and stronger than the familiar fable.
:125. The obscure sequence that produced QWERTY may never be reconstructed with a single agreed explanation.
:126. The mechanisms that helped preserve its descendants are much easier to see.
:127. Learned motor patterns made switching personally costly, widespread compatibility made the established layout collectively useful, and expectations about other users reinforced both effects.
:128. None of those mechanisms requires a villain, a conspiracy, or a foolish consumer.
:129. Nor do they require the original arrangement to have been the worst one available.
:130. They require only that adoption today alter the relative cost of adoption tomorrow.
:131. A brief comparison with other standards confirms the pattern without making every standard another keyboard.
:132. Railway gauges bind through track, rolling stock, stations, and freight connections, while electrical systems bind through generators, appliances, wiring, and safety rules.
:133. Keyboard layouts bind far more heavily through acquired human skill and coordination than through immovable capital.
:134. Because the carriers of the past differ, the cost and method of change differ as well.
:135. The keyboard's lighter infrastructure leaves room for gradual migration, optional layouts, bilingual users, and new input methods.
:136. That remaining flexibility matters because lock-in means a raised cost of transition, not an impossibility of movement.
:137. Return now to the sheet of glass.
:138. Its software can redraw every key in an instant, yet it usually opens with the arrangement inherited from the mechanical age.
:139. Keeping that arrangement saves billions of users from relearning a basic interface and lets them move among devices with little preparation.
:140. The same benefit reduces the reward for developing, teaching, labeling, and supporting alternatives.
:141. Stability and constraint are produced by the same compatibility.
:142. The dead typebars do not reach forward and command the touchscreen.
:143. They helped select a starting arrangement from which training, manufacture, institutions, and expectations accumulated.
:144. History did not make every later keyboard inevitable, but it changed which keyboards were cheap, familiar, visible, and worth coordinating around.
:145. The machine forgot its old limitation long ago, while the system built around its solution kept remembering.
