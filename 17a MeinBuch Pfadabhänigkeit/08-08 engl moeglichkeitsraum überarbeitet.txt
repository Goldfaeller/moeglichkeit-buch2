== 8.8 Die Grenzen der freundlichen Lesart ==

:1. In 1974, an East German citizen opening the republic’s newly revised constitution could find two promises within a few pages of each other.
:2. Article 20 declared that women and men had equal rights and the same legal status in every sphere of public, governmental, and personal life.
:3. Article 1 located political leadership in the working class and its Marxist-Leninist party, a formula that constitutionalized the ruling Socialist Unity Party’s supremacy.
:4. Both provisions described something real about the German Democratic Republic, and reading either one while ignoring the other produces a distorted history.
:5. The equality clause expressed a program that had already altered education, employment, family law, and everyday expectations for millions of women.
:6. The leadership clause identified the boundary those changes were not permitted to cross: citizens could receive new entitlements without acquiring an independent power to define, enforce, or revise them against the party-state.
:7. This juxtaposition exposes the danger of judging an entire legal order by its most attractive rules.
:8. A statute can be progressive in what it grants, an institution can be effective in delivering part of that grant, and the surrounding system can still deny the legal safeguards by which a right becomes a shield against government.
:9. Those are not contradictory verdicts because they refer to different layers of law.
:10. The hard task is to keep all the layers visible at once.
:11. Consider women’s paid work, one of the strongest cases for a friendly reading of East German social policy.
:12. The first East German constitution had proclaimed equality in 1949, and the state subsequently built employment, training, maternity protection, and extensive public child care around the expectation that women would earn wages.
:13. By the 1980s, more than nine in ten women of working age were employed or in education, a level that made the working mother ordinary rather than exceptional.
:14. Nurseries, kindergartens, school-day care, workplace services, and legally protected employment did more than decorate an ideological claim.
:15. They changed the feasible choices facing a woman with children by reducing the time, risk, and private expense attached to holding a job.
:16. That is a genuine expansion of a possibility space.
:17. It also created durable skills, pension claims, workplace relationships, and expectations of economic independence that did not vanish when the state that had supported them disappeared.
:18. Describing these gains as mere propaganda would repeat the very mistake a path analysis is meant to prevent: mistaking the origin or motive of an institution for the full range of its effects.
:19. Socialist commitments mattered, but so did labor scarcity, production targets, demographic concerns, and the government’s desire to draw nearly every able adult into the organized economy.
:20. A policy may serve citizens and state capacity at the same time.
:21. Yet the institutional package was designed less around redistributing care between women and men than around enabling women to add paid employment to responsibilities they largely retained.
:22. An internal East German time-use study for 1970 attributed roughly thirty-seven of forty-seven weekly hours of household labor to women.
:23. Even with broad child-care coverage, women commonly organized shopping, appointments, meals, and the daily movement of children between home and public institutions.
:24. They consequently had substantially less leisure than men, while shortages could turn routine provisioning into an additional drain on time.
:25. The formal worker was therefore not genderless; the favored social figure was often the worker-mother.
:26. Benefits aimed specifically at mothers eased a real burden, but they could also stabilize the assumption that the burden was maternal in the first place.
:27. This is path dependence through complementarity: child-care institutions, workplace rules, family benefits, and inherited domestic norms fitted together well enough to sustain high female employment without forcing an equal division of unpaid work.
:28. Once that arrangement became normal, reforming one element did not automatically transform the others.
:29. The same pattern appeared in careers.
:30. Women entered skilled occupations and many middle-level positions, yet they remained rarer toward the top of industrial and political hierarchies, where advancement required time, party reliability, and access to networks still dominated by men.
:31. Average earnings also remained lower, despite the legal principle of equal pay for equal work, partly because jobs, sectors, shifts, bonuses, and senior positions were distributed unequally.
:32. None of this cancels the value of wages, qualifications, or child care.
:33. It changes the proposition being tested.
:34. If equality means access to employment, the East German record contains substantial achievement; if it means equal command of time, comparable advancement, and equal influence over the rules, the record is much weaker.
:35. The distinction is not a semantic escape hatch but a causal map of where an entitlement was supported and where its path stopped.
:36. It also prevents comparison with West Germany from becoming a contest in which one favorable statistic settles every question.
:37. West Germany’s Basic Law declared women and men equal in 1949, but much of the inherited civil law continued to encode a male breadwinner and female homemaker.
:38. Parliament missed the constitutional deadline for bringing family law into conformity, and the Equal Rights Act did not pass until 1957.
:39. Even that reform retained a paternal casting vote over children until the Federal Constitutional Court rejected it in 1959.
:40. The legally prescribed division of marital roles disappeared only with the marriage and family-law reform that took effect in 1977.
:41. Here the western path shows its own split between constitutional language and social practice.
:42. It was not a smooth march from liberal principle to lived equality, and pressure from women’s organizations, litigants, changing labor markets, political conflict, and constitutional adjudication was necessary to move it.
:43. The comparison therefore yields no honest sequence in which every eastern reform was cosmetic and every western reform was the natural unfolding of democracy.
:44. What differed most sharply was not whether written law ever lagged behind reality, because it did so in both states, but what citizens could do when official claims and government action diverged.
:45. In the Federal Republic, the equality clause became directly binding law within a plural political order, and litigants could bring disputes before courts empowered to invalidate legislation.
:46. That machinery did not guarantee prompt justice, as the long survival of discriminatory rules demonstrates, but it created an authorized route by which a constitutional promise could disrupt the ordinary legal path.
:47. East German constitutions also used the language of judicial independence.
:48. The institutional setting gave that language a different force.
:49. The system rejected a separation of powers, judges were elected for terms and accountable within a centralized structure, and there was no independent constitutional court through which a citizen could strike down a statute.
:50. Judicial review of administrative action was absent for most of the republic’s history and appeared only in a narrow form near its end.
:51. The point should not be exaggerated into the claim that every East German lawsuit was decided by a party official.
:52. Courts, workplace conflict commissions, and neighborhood arbitration bodies processed large numbers of civil, family, labor, and minor criminal disputes, many of which left legal professionals meaningful room to resolve ordinary conflicts.
:53. Predictable rules governing a purchase, a divorce, maintenance, or a workplace disagreement were necessary to daily life, and the state could not run them all as political theater.
:54. That ordinary functionality helps explain why citizens could experience law as useful in one encounter and defenseless in another.
:55. The decisive boundary appeared when a dispute touched the ruling party’s authority, the security apparatus, unauthorized organization, public dissent, or the right to leave.
:56. There the written independence of judges collided with recruitment, training, career control, centralized guidance, and the expectation of socialist partisanship.
:57. Law ceased to stand outside political power as a check upon it and instead became one of the instruments through which political power classified opposition and imposed consequences.
:58. The mechanism was deeper than occasional corruption by a telephone call from above.
:59. It lay in the complementary design of party leadership, prosecutorial supervision, judicial administration, restricted advocacy, political criminal offenses, and secret-police investigation.
:60. To see what that design did to apparently liberal reforms, the comparison must move from women’s work and family policy into the especially revealing terrain where private conduct, public organization, and political loyalty met.
:61. The law governing homosexuality provides such a test because East German reform was both consequential and sharply bounded.
:62. In 1950, East German courts rejected the Nazi expansion of Paragraph 175 and returned to the narrower version that had preceded it, although consensual sex between adult men remained punishable.
:63. The criminal code introduced in 1968 no longer criminalized consensual same-sex relations between adults.
:64. Its Paragraph 151 nevertheless imposed a higher age threshold on same-sex conduct than on heterosexual conduct and applied to women as well as men.
:65. The legislature repealed that unequal provision in 1988, with the change taking effect in 1989.
:66. West Germany did not decriminalize consensual relations between adult men until its 1969 reform, retained further discriminatory provisions for years, and finally removed Paragraph 175 in 1994.
:67. On this dimension, the state whose legal order disappeared in 1990 had left the older punitive path sooner than the state whose law became dominant.
:68. That fact resists any theory that assigns every liberal outcome to the western political system and every regressive outcome to the eastern one.
:69. It does not establish the reverse theory.
:70. Decriminalizing private conduct removed a grave threat, but it did not give lesbian and gay citizens secure freedom to publish, assemble, build independent organizations, or challenge official policy.
:71. Activists sometimes found limited shelter in Protestant churches during the 1980s, and changing official attitudes created openings, yet autonomous organization remained subject to surveillance and political control.
:72. The state could therefore widen one corridor of private life while keeping the doors to collective action narrow.
:73. That distinction matters because durable rights movements need more than tolerance from administrators; they need communication, association, public argument, and institutions able to resist a reversal.
:74. A legal reform may improve lives even when its beneficiaries cannot safely organize to claim authorship of the change or determine its next step.
:75. Calling that reform unreal would deny its practical effect, while calling it freedom would ignore who controlled its scope.
:76. East Germany’s 1972 abortion law presents a related but not identical pattern.
:77. It allowed a woman to decide on an abortion during the first twelve weeks of pregnancy and made the procedure part of public health provision.
:78. The rule expanded bodily and economic options more directly than a proclamation of equality alone could do.
:79. It also emerged from a state that combined women’s employment, family policy, demographic planning, and medical administration into a single governing project.
:80. The sole dissenting votes in the normally unanimous People’s Chamber came from members of the Christian Democratic bloc, a rare visible disagreement that revealed both genuine moral conflict and the chamber’s usual lack of open contestation.
:81. West Germany’s path again defeats a simple chronology of superior liberalization.
:82. Parliament adopted a first-trimester model in 1974, but the Federal Constitutional Court invalidated central parts of it in 1975, and the 1976 settlement returned to an indications-based system.
:83. Constitutional review thus protected a conception of unborn life while narrowing the choice that a parliamentary majority had sought to give pregnant women.
:84. The same institution that could unlock inherited discrimination could also close a newly opened route.
:85. Independent review is a mechanism of contestable constraint, not a machine that automatically produces socially liberal results.
:86. Its democratic value lies in reasoned, legally bounded opposition to power and in the possibility of continued challenge, rather than in guaranteeing that every decision points in one ideological direction.
:87. The East German contrast lay less in the existence of official reasons than in the absence of an independent forum capable of making the ruling party answer to a rival interpretation of higher law.
:88. That absence became unmistakable when citizens challenged the reported results of the May 1989 local elections.
:89. After observers documented discrepancies and allegations of fraud spread, the state did not permit an ordinary legal inquiry to follow the evidence wherever it led.
:90. The security leadership instructed officials to receive complaints and later answer that no indication of a criminal offense had been found.
:91. Here the enforcement chain ran backward from the desired political conclusion to the legal response.
:92. The episode was not representative of every maintenance dispute or divorce, but it was diagnostic of the system at precisely the point where legality threatened ruling authority.
:93. Political criminal proceedings could be prepared by the Ministry for State Security and coordinated with senior party officials, leaving defense lawyers and judges little room to alter predetermined outcomes.
:94. Offenses framed around hostile agitation, public order, unauthorized border crossing, or attacks on the state converted political boundaries into criminal classifications.
:95. Surveillance extended the effect beyond those eventually prosecuted, because the possibility of observation changed the expected cost of speaking, meeting, corresponding, or applying to leave.
:96. This was path dependence generated by organized anticipation.
:97. Past interventions taught citizens what attracted attention, while informant networks, personnel files, professional sanctions, and prosecutorial practice made future caution rational even without a fresh command in every case.
:98. The resulting silence could then be cited as evidence of consent, helping the political order reproduce the appearance on which its legitimacy claims depended.
:99. Private adaptation supplied many people with room for friendship, work, family, humor, and quiet nonconformity, so the history cannot be reduced to continuous terror.
:100. Yet an ability to carve out ordinary life is not equivalent to a legally protected ability to oppose the state.
:101. The serious rival explanation for East German legal change is that material necessity, not emancipatory principle, did most of the work.
:102. Labor shortages plainly encouraged female employment, demographic goals shaped family policy, and the party’s ideological objectives influenced sexual and reproductive regulation.
:103. Motives, however, do not exhaust mechanisms or consequences.
:104. A nursery built partly to meet a production plan still gave parents usable hours, just as a repeal enacted from above still removed a criminal penalty.
:105. The stronger criticism asks who defined the benefit, whether recipients could refuse its attached expectations, and what remedy existed when administration turned support into pressure.
:106. This separates paternal provision from enforceable citizenship without pretending that provision has no value.
:107. It also places the West German record under the same analytical discipline.
:108. Market demand could draw women into employment while tax rules, limited child care, and the male-breadwinner model pushed in the other direction; constitutional rights could enable challenges while inherited statutes and judicial interpretations delayed change.
:109. Neither state possessed a single master variable called progress.
:110. Each combined legal text, administrative capacity, social norms, economic incentives, organized interests, and avenues of appeal, but the combinations differed profoundly in who could contest them.
:111. A fair comparison therefore remains asymmetric without becoming one-sided.
:112. The absence of free elections, independent courts, and protected opposition in East Germany is not balanced away by child-care places or an earlier penal reform, because these features answer different questions about power.
:113. Conversely, authoritarian rule does not turn every school place, employment guarantee, divorce rule, or medical entitlement into an illusion.
:114. Moral judgment becomes more accurate when it follows the causal layers instead of compressing them into a national score.
:115. At the level of an isolated rule, change may require only a legislative deletion.
:116. At the level of daily capability, it may require buildings, trained staff, budgets, employers, and expectations accumulated over decades.
:117. At the level of citizenship, it also requires institutions through which people can challenge the officials who interpret all those arrangements.
:118. These layers travel with different ease when a political order breaks.
:119. A statute can be repealed overnight, a professional culture retrained only slowly, and a habit of caution may outlive the office that once made caution prudent.
:120. When the two German legal paths met in 1990, the decisive question was therefore not which side possessed the longer list of admirable provisions, but which rules were portable, which institutions carried their own supporting networks, and who had the power and time to choose between them.
