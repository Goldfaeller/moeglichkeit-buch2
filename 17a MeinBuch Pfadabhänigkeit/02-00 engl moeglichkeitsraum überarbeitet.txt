== 2. Der Umbau bei laufendem Betrieb ==

:1. Consider what it takes to renovate a house while the family goes on living in it.
:2. Nobody moves out, because there is nowhere else to go.
:3. The roof cannot come off in October and go back on in March.
:4. The water has to run every evening, the stairs have to hold every morning, and every night everyone has to sleep somewhere dry.
:5. So the work proceeds room by room, in a sequence dictated less by an ideal floor plan than by what the household can survive.
:6. The new bathroom ends up beside the old chimney because the pipes are already there.
:7. One doorway is walled up and another cut six feet away, while the outline of the first remains visible in the plaster.
:8. A staircase that once led to a hayloft now leads to a bedroom because removing it would have left no way upstairs.
:9. After thirty years the house works perfectly well, and it is also unmistakably odd.
:10. Show the floor plan to an architect who has never seen the place, and its present use will not explain every peculiarity.
:11. Some features become intelligible only when the sequence of renovations is known.
:12. The analogy has a limit that matters from the start.
:13. A house has builders who can imagine an outcome, whereas evolution has no foresight, blueprint, or renovation committee.
:14. Organisms are also not machines assembled from separable parts with one purpose apiece.
:15. Yet the inhabited house captures one severe condition shared by every lineage: change occurs in a system whose bearers must continue living and reproducing.
:16. The construction site is therefore older, larger, and stranger than any house.
:17. Life has existed on Earth for more than three and a half billion years, although the evidence becomes harder to interpret the farther back researchers look.
:18. Every living organism descends through an unbroken chain of organisms that reproduced.
:19. A break anywhere in that particular chain would mean that the descendant at its end did not exist.
:20. Evolution can therefore never do what an engineer may do in a factory: stop production, clear the site, and restart from a clean design.
:21. There is no clean site waiting behind life.
:22. There are inherited bodies, developmental processes, genes, environments, and populations that change while life continues.
:23. This continuity does not explain everything unusual about organisms, but it changes the questions that a good explanation must ask.
:24. We often begin with the opposite kind of object.
:25. A deliberately designed machine can be planned backward from a stated purpose.
:26. Its makers may select materials and arrange parts so that they contribute directly to that purpose.
:27. Even machines inherit standards, tools, and earlier designs, but designers can sometimes discard an arrangement wholesale in a way that no ancestral population can.
:28. This makes engineered objects appear more legible from present function than living bodies do.
:29. Open a body and you find structures that perform several tasks, structures whose routes seem indirect, and remnants whose present effects are modest or context-dependent.
:30. Earlier observers often treated such features either as embarrassments to be explained away or as products of a designer with peculiar taste.
:31. They are better read as historical evidence.
:32. A body does not simply reveal what its parts do now; it also preserves traces of how those parts became available.
:33. Function and history are different explanations, and neither can automatically replace the other.
:34. A trait may work well because selection favored it in a particular environment.
:35. It may also have its particular form because development could modify an inherited structure more readily in one direction than another.
:36. The central puzzle is not why life contains imperfection, as though evolution had failed an engineering exam.
:37. The puzzle is how earlier forms alter the set, probability, and cost of forms that can arise later.
:38. Deep time makes those inherited effects difficult to feel.
:39. If the history of life were compressed into a calendar year, recorded human history would occupy only the closing moments of December 31.
:40. The exact calendar dates assigned to evolutionary events would shift with the chosen estimate and scale, but the disproportion would remain.
:41. Structures inherited from ancient aquatic vertebrates still contribute to the organization of a human skull, spine, limbs, nerves, and organs.
:42. That does not mean a fish once settled the human body plan in a single evolutionary decision.
:43. It means that later vertebrates developed through repeated modifications of earlier developmental and anatomical relationships.
:44. The familiar one-bone, two-bone, many-bone pattern in a human arm belongs to a much older history of paired appendages.
:45. Similar inherited elements have since been modified into the forelimbs of bats, whales, horses, and humans.
:46. The common pattern persisted while proportions, tissues, and uses multiplied.
:47. Persistence is not stasis.
:48. Continents moved, climates changed, lineages disappeared, and the fossil record registers several exceptionally severe episodes of extinction.
:49. Through those disruptions, some inherited arrangements vanished and others survived long enough to be elaborated in new settings.
:50. The survivors were not necessarily the forms that would have been best under every future condition.
:51. They were the forms that existed, reproduced, and passed a workable starting point onward.
:52. That difference between the conceivable and the historically available is the biological core of path dependence.
:53. The mechanism begins with variation.
:54. Natural selection does not summon a useful feature because a population needs it.
:55. Heritable differences must first arise through processes such as mutation and recombination, or enter through gene flow, before selection can change their representation in a population.
:56. Those differences are not a uniform sample of every form that physics would permit.
:57. Mutation rates vary across genomes, and developmental systems turn some genetic changes into phenotypic variation more readily than others.
:58. Calling variation undirected means that useful changes are not produced in response to an organism's future requirements.
:59. It does not mean that every possible change is equally likely.
:60. Selection changes the representation of heritable variants when those variants are associated with different reproductive success in a particular environment.
:61. In that immediate sense, selection is a filter and amplifier rather than the author of whatever variation might be imagined.
:62. If a helpful variant is absent, selection cannot commission it.
:63. If several helpful variants are present, their effects may depend on the rest of the organism and on how common they are.
:64. A population facing a new predator does not order thicker armor.
:65. It may adapt if relevant variation already exists or arises in time, but it may instead move, decline, persist without genetic change, or disappear.
:66. This is why the phrase “survival of the fittest” so easily misleads.
:67. Fitness in evolutionary biology concerns reproductive success in a specified environment, not a medal for the best organism that could ever be designed.
:68. The competitors are not all conceivable organisms but the variants actually present in populations at particular times.
:69. Even among those variants, selection is not the only force changing their frequencies.
:70. Genetic drift can spread or eliminate variants through chance sampling, especially in small populations.
:71. Migration can import variants, recombination can rearrange them, and environmental change can reverse which combinations are favored.
:72. Evolution is consequently neither pure optimization nor pure accident.
:73. It is the joint result of biased supplies of variation, inheritance, selection, drift, movement, development, and changing ecological conditions.
:74. Existing organization shapes every one of those processes.
:75. A copying change can alter a protein, duplicate a stretch of DNA, change gene regulation, or sometimes have no detectable effect.
:76. Larger novelties emerge through sequences of such changes interacting with development, not as complete organs appearing in a single leap.
:77. The raw material is therefore inherited material, but inheritance can be rearranged, multiplied, reduced, and recruited for new work.
:78. A second part of the mechanism gives this path a shape that can be pictured.
:79. Sewall Wright introduced the adaptive-landscape image in the early 1930s while explaining how evolutionary forces act on populations.
:80. In its simplest popular form, the image resembles a country of hills and valleys.
:81. Nearby positions represent variants reachable by small changes, while height represents reproductive success under stated conditions.
:82. Peaks are combinations with higher fitness than their immediate neighbors, and valleys are combinations with lower fitness.
:83. A population is not a solitary hiker surveying this country from above.
:84. It contains many individuals and variants, none of which can inspect distant alternatives or choose a destination.
:85. Under simplified conditions, selection tends to increase the frequency of variants that lie uphill from their neighbors.
:86. A modest local peak can then matter even if a higher peak exists elsewhere.
:87. Reaching the higher peak may require intermediate combinations that leave fewer descendants in the current environment.
:88. Strong selection against those intermediates can make the distant peak difficult to reach from the population's present position.
:89. The better distant form is not physically impossible, yet it may be historically inaccessible by the small viable steps available at that time.
:90. This is one way that a possibility can exist in theory and remain absent in practice.
:91. The path already taken is not a prison wall, however, and the landscape is not a literal geography.
:92. Drift can sometimes carry populations across a shallow fitness depression, particularly when populations are small.
:93. Recombination, migration, gene duplication, and environmental change can create routes that the simple picture omits.
:94. A temporarily harmful change may also persist because it is linked to a beneficial one or because selection is weak relative to chance.
:95. Evolution therefore does not move only uphill, even though hill climbing captures one important effect of selection in simple models.
:96. This qualification strengthens the argument about path dependence because it identifies a mechanism rather than turning a metaphor into a law.
:97. The landscape has further limits.
:98. Genotype, phenotype, and fitness are distinct, although a flat drawing can blur them together.
:99. The same genotype can produce different phenotypes in different environments, and the same phenotype can have different fitness when conditions or competitors change.
:100. Real spaces of genetic and phenotypic variation have many dimensions, and adjacency in one representation may conceal a route visible in another.
:101. Fitness can also depend on frequency, so the success of a variant may change as the variant becomes common.
:102. The terrain itself moves when climates shift, prey evolve, pathogens adapt, or potential mates respond differently.
:103. Organisms also alter the conditions under which selection later acts by consuming resources, building structures, changing soils, or modifying the abundance of other species.
:104. This process, often called niche construction, does not give organisms control over evolution.
:105. It means that activity in one generation can help create the selective environment inherited by the next.
:106. The landscape is thus best kept as a disciplined sketch of local reachability, not treated as a measurable mountain range already waiting for life to explore.
:107. Its value lies in separating three questions that casual talk about adaptation tends to merge.
:108. A form may be physically possible, it may function well if completed, and it may still lack an accessible sequence of intermediate forms from here.
:109. Path dependence enters through that phrase “from here.”
:110. A third part of the mechanism is sequence dependence.
:111. Every inherited change may alter the effects of later changes.
:112. A duplicated gene, for example, can create room for one copy to change while another continues an existing function.
:113. A structure recruited for an additional task may come under several selective demands at once.
:114. A variant that helps in one genetic background can be neutral or harmful in another because genes and proteins interact.
:115. Biologists call such context-dependent genetic effects epistasis.
:116. With sign epistasis, a mutation can even change from beneficial to harmful when another mutation is present.
:117. Order then becomes causal rather than merely chronological.
:118. Change A may make change B tolerable, while B arriving before A may be eliminated.
:119. Two lineages exposed to similar conditions can therefore take different routes because their inherited backgrounds make different variants available or acceptable.
:120. Conversely, strong similar pressures can sometimes produce convergence, with separate lineages arriving at similar functions by different routes.
:121. Convergence is a useful warning against fatalism, but it does not erase the differences in anatomy, development, or future options beneath the shared function.
:122. History changes probabilities without dictating every outcome.
:123. This distinction separates contingency from arbitrariness.
:124. A contingent step could have gone differently, yet whatever follows remains constrained by real chemistry, development, ecology, and selection.
:125. It also separates path dependence from a general physical limit.
:126. Gravity constrains every large land animal whether or not its lineage encountered a particular earlier fork.
:127. A constraint becomes path-dependent when the order of earlier states changes which later forms are reachable, likely, or costly from otherwise relevant starting points.
:128. A fourth part of the mechanism is the growth of dependency.
:129. Building on an inherited structure can be easier than removing it cleanly.
:130. A feature that initially contributes to one process may later interact with muscles, nerves, regulatory genes, behaviors, or ecological partners.
:131. Further changes evolve in the presence of those interactions and may come to rely on them.
:132. The original feature is then no longer an isolated component but a junction in a network of development and function.
:133. Removing it may require several coordinated changes, and intermediate combinations must still develop, survive, and reproduce well enough to persist.
:134. Scaffolding, in other words, can become load-bearing.
:135. This is a biological form of complementary investment.
:136. Each additional dependency may increase the cost of returning to an earlier arrangement, even when no single dependency makes return impossible.
:137. Trait loss itself can happen in several ways.
:138. Selection may favor losing an expensive structure, relaxed selection may permit mutations to degrade it, or developmental changes may reduce it as a side effect.
:139. A reduced remnant can persist when eliminating its last traces offers little reproductive advantage.
:140. After genes, regulatory interactions, and developmental contexts have changed, exact reconstruction of an ancestral state becomes increasingly improbable.
:141. This tendency is sometimes associated with Dollo's law, but it is not an exceptionless ban on the reappearance of lost traits.
:142. Similar forms and functions can re-evolve, and some developmental capacities can remain latent long after a visible feature disappears.
:143. What is especially difficult is a precise return to the same state through the same historical network of relations.
:144. Irreversibility is therefore usually a matter of accumulated context, not a mysterious one-way force.
:145. The same accumulation can preserve benefits as well as burdens.
:146. A dependable developmental arrangement coordinates many processes and allows later changes to build on something that already works.
:147. A shared body plan does not merely exclude alternatives; it supplies stable material for diversification.
:148. The inherited pattern of a vertebrate forelimb has supported walking, digging, swimming, flying, grasping, and manipulation.
:149. Its continuity is not evidence that all those uses were latent goals.
:150. It shows how a reliable structure can become a platform for innovations that were not available before the structure existed.
:151. A trait that arose or was shaped in one context may later contribute to a different function, a process known as exaptation.
:152. Such reuse prevents path dependence from becoming a catalogue of design flaws.
:153. History can close routes, but it can also furnish parts, interactions, and developmental capacities that open new ones.
:154. The relevant question is always what the previous path does to the next neighborhood of possibilities.
:155. Sometimes it narrows that neighborhood by making alternatives costly or developmentally inaccessible.
:156. Sometimes it enlarges the neighborhood by duplicating a gene, modularizing a structure, or placing an old feature in a new environment.
:157. Often it does both at once.
:158. This double effect is why present performance and evolvability must be kept separate.
:159. A trait can perform its current task efficiently while leaving few tolerable routes for modification.
:160. Another arrangement can be less efficient now yet preserve variation, redundancy, or modularity that becomes useful after conditions change.
:161. Evolvability is not a promise of progress and cannot be measured by one universal yardstick.
:162. It is a context-dependent capacity to generate and retain heritable changes that may become useful.
:163. Selection does not consistently maximize that future capacity, because immediate reproductive differences can outweigh benefits that might appear only under later conditions.
:164. Nor does every unused option deserve to be preserved, since maintaining redundancy and flexibility can itself consume resources.
:165. The biological possibility space is therefore structured by trade-offs as well as by history.
:166. A trade-off becomes path-dependent when an earlier compromise changes the costs or availability of later compromises.
:167. Without that historical mechanism, a present constraint or competing demand is not enough to establish path dependence.
:168. The distinction matters when interpreting apparently awkward anatomy.
:169. Some features record ancestry, some reflect present mechanical limits, some balance competing functions, and many combine all three.
:170. A persuasive historical explanation must identify which earlier relationship was inherited and how it altered later variation or transition costs.
:171. Merely pointing to an odd structure and declaring that evolution is imperfect explains very little.
:172. The detailed cases of nerves, spines, eyes, proteins, mutations, and long-running populations become valuable only when they reveal one of these mechanisms rather than decorate a story about bad design.
:173. Together they test an account that can now be stated without treating evolution as an engineer.
:174. Variation arrives unevenly from inherited systems; selection and drift alter what persists; each change modifies the effects of later changes; and dependencies make some reversals increasingly expensive.
:175. None of those processes requires a plan.
:176. None guarantees that the most efficient conceivable organism will appear.
:177. None implies that lineages are frozen.
:178. Their combined effect is subtler: the route already traveled changes the distribution of routes that remain.
:179. That is why present function alone can leave a body partly unexplained.
:180. A direct route on an anatomist's diagram may have been inaccessible to a lineage whose development had to keep producing viable organisms at every generation.
:181. An apparent detour may persist because other structures have grown around it, while an inherited component may survive because it performs several useful tasks at once.
:182. The body is neither an optimal machine nor a heap of accidents.
:183. It is a working record of constrained continuity, differential reproduction, chance retention, and repeated reuse.
:184. Its peculiarities show what history costs, while its innovations show what history supplies.
:185. Life's oldest renovation project has never reached a final floor plan, because every workable room becomes part of the site on which the next change must be built.
