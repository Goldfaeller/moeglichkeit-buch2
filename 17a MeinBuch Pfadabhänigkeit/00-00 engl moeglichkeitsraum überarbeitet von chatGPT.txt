== Einleitung zum Buch ==

:1. Somewhere in your body, a nerve takes a long detour to cover a short straight-line distance.
:2. In most people, fibers that help control the larynx descend from the brainstem within the vagus nerve, travel toward the chest, and turn beneath major arteries before rising toward the throat.
:3. On the left side, the recurrent laryngeal nerve loops under the arch of the aorta.
:4. In a giraffe, that inherited route can extend for several meters to reach a destination only a small fraction of that distance from the brain.
:5. Drawn as a cable on an empty page, the route looks needlessly extravagant.
:6. In a living body, however, the loop is neither an idle cord nor a joke at evolution's expense, because the nerve carries essential motor and sensory signals and gives off branches along its course.
:7. Its striking detour is a consequence of how nerves, blood vessels, the heart, and the neck developed in relation to one another.
:8. Deep in vertebrate ancestry, the corresponding nerve and artery lay close together in the pharyngeal region.
:9. As later bodies changed, the old topological relationship persisted while the distance between the structures grew.
:10. Mammalian embryos rebuild that relationship as their arteries and organs take shape, so an adult's anatomy contains developmental history as well as evolutionary history.
:11. Natural selection can modify inherited development, but it does not suspend an organism in midair and reconnect every part from scratch.
:12. A shorter adult route is easy to sketch, yet reaching it through viable heritable changes is a different problem.
:13. Rare nonrecurrent nerves in humans show that another route is anatomically possible, but such variants are usually associated with altered blood-vessel development rather than with a nerve simply jumping across an artery.
:14. The nerve is therefore not merely old.
:15. Earlier relationships among its parts have changed which later arrangements are readily reachable.
:16. That distinction opens the central question of this book.
:17. How do earlier states, decisions, and sequences change the number, visibility, probability, and cost of later possibilities?
:18. The name for this family of effects is path dependence.
:19. The phrase is often weakened until it means little more than the unsurprising claim that the past matters.
:20. A useful definition must demand more.
:21. A process is path dependent when the route taken changes what can happen next, how likely it is to happen, or what a transition will cost.
:22. Sequence matters because steps alter the system that must take the following steps.
:23. An early choice may create skills, machines, categories, allies, expectations, or physical structures that favor some continuations over others.
:24. It may also erase evidence that an abandoned continuation was ever plausible.
:25. History alone is not enough.
:26. A fair coin has a history of earlier tosses, but if neither the coin nor the procedure has changed, that sequence does not alter the probabilities of the next toss.
:27. A mountain blocks a road whether travelers arrived from the east or the west, so the mountain is a present physical constraint rather than a path-dependent one.
:28. Limited fuel creates a trade-off between speed and range even in a vehicle with no meaningful history.
:29. These cases have pasts, limits, and costs, but they do not yet show a path shaping its own continuation.
:30. Path dependence begins where the sequence itself changes the later field of action.
:31. The idea becomes easier to handle if we draw a map, provided we remember that the map is an analytical model rather than a place hidden behind the world.
:32. Imagine every possible version of a system represented as a point.
:33. Nearby points differ by changes the system can make without first becoming something else entirely.
:34. A human body, a railway network, a legal code, and a software platform would each require a different map, because their states and transitions are not comparable in any simple unit.
:35. Theoretically thinkable possibilities form the widest set, including many arrangements that can be described in words but cannot exist.
:36. Physical possibility removes whatever violates material laws and resource limits.
:37. Functional possibility removes states that cannot perform the minimum task required to persist.
:38. Historical accessibility narrows the set again to states that can be reached from the present one through available transitions.
:39. A bridge that can support traffic once complete may still be inaccessible if no feasible construction sequence can keep the river crossing usable during the work.
:40. A biological change that would function in its finished form may remain inaccessible if the necessary intermediate forms cannot survive and reproduce.
:41. A system does not float above this imagined terrain and select any destination it can name.
:42. It begins somewhere, and every step must leave it capable of taking another.
:43. Some regions are close, others require costly passages, and still others have no known route from the present position.
:44. This is the possibility space.
:45. What matters is not only how many points the model contains but which points are connected from here.
:46. Where a system stands depends partly on earlier steps, and those steps may also have changed the connections around it.
:47. The landscape image has limits, because real possibility spaces are rarely fixed, visible, or measurable in all their dimensions.
:48. Innovation can create a new bridge, an environmental change can erase an old one, and a new interface can make formerly separate regions adjacent.
:49. Some possibilities are lost because the capacities needed to reach them disappear, while others are merely hidden because current categories no longer make them easy to imagine.
:50. Still others become available only after an earlier innovation supplies the missing intermediate step.
:51. A map of possibilities must therefore include time, because both the traveler and the terrain can change.
:52. This is where contingency enters the argument.
:53. Contingency means that events took one possible course without having been compelled to take exactly that course.
:54. It does not mean that every outcome was equally possible or that outcomes appeared without causes.
:55. Nor does it make every accident important.
:56. Most small disturbances are damped, corrected, forgotten, or overwhelmed by stronger forces.
:57. A contingent event becomes historically powerful only when some mechanism preserves or amplifies its effects.
:58. Positive feedback provides one such mechanism when an early advantage creates conditions that make further advantage more likely.
:59. Practice can improve performance, greater scale can lower unit costs, a larger network can attract more users, and shared expectations can direct investment toward the option expected to win.
:60. These mechanisms are related, but they are not interchangeable.
:61. A beaver alters a river by building a dam, and later generations encounter a pond partly produced by earlier activity.
:62. Human systems likewise build parts of their own future environment through roads, standards, curricula, databases, and laws.
:63. Positive feedback is not positive in the sense of good, and no real reinforcing process grows without limits.
:64. Congestion, scarcity, competition, regulation, error correction, and changing demand can slow or reverse it.
:65. When reinforcement persists long enough, it can produce lock-in.
:66. Lock-in is not a magical seal but a condition in which leaving a path has become expensive because many complementary parts now depend on it.
:67. The binding force may reside less in the original object than in the training, equipment, contracts, habits, and neighboring systems assembled around it.
:68. Money already spent cannot be recovered, but the more important burden is often the future cost of replacing what still works.
:69. Lock-in comes in degrees, and high switching cost is not the same as absolute impossibility.
:70. The stability it creates may be the main reason a system became valuable.
:71. A common language, a dependable safety standard, and a compatible payment network allow strangers to coordinate without renegotiating every interaction.
:72. Their widespread adoption saves time, prevents error, and makes long-term investment possible.
:73. The same web of benefits can make an improved alternative difficult to introduce.
:74. A lock-in can therefore be useful and change-inhibiting at the same time.
:75. Dominance does not prove technical superiority, but persistence does not prove that a system is an absurd historical mistake either.
:76. Both judgments require comparison across several goals.
:77. A racing bicycle may be better for speed while a cargo bicycle is better for carrying groceries, and neither difference requires path dependence.
:78. That is a trade-off, a conflict among objectives that cannot all be maximized with the same limited resources and design.
:79. A trade-off becomes path dependent when an earlier compromise directs later investment, expertise, and improvement along one architecture rather than another.
:80. Years spent optimizing speed may remove the spare capacity that would have made later adaptation cheap.
:81. Present performance and future changeability are different achievements.
:82. Evolvability is the capacity to keep changing without destroying the system's ability to function.
:83. It is not identical to high performance, short-term flexibility, robustness against one known shock, or recovery after damage.
:84. Modular parts, redundant capacities, diverse approaches, open interfaces, and tolerance for small failures can preserve more reachable neighbors in a possibility space.
:85. Each of those features also carries a present cost, which is why evolvability can look like waste until the environment changes.
:86. Biology makes this tension unusually vivid because living systems cannot pause reproduction while a new design is installed.
:87. Evolution resembles the renovation of an occupied building only in this limited respect, because no crew plans the work and no blueprint defines the destination.
:88. Mutation and recombination generate variation, while selection, drift, development, and changing environments influence which variations persist.
:89. Every lineage inherits an already integrated organism, and viable descendants arise through modifications of that inheritance.
:90. The human spine was not simply a fish's horizontal backbone stood on end, but upright locomotion did evolve by remodeling structures inherited through a long vertebrate and primate history.
:91. Human childbirth likewise reflects several interacting constraints involving fetal size, pelvic form, pelvic-floor support, metabolism, development, ecology, and culture, not one tidy bargain between a large brain and efficient walking.
:92. The vertebrate retina places neural tissue in front of its photoreceptors and routes the optic nerve through the retinal surface, creating a blind spot.
:93. Coleoid cephalopods reached a camera-like eye through a different developmental history and have noninverted retinas without the same anatomical blind spot.
:94. This contrast does not establish a simple ranking of good and bad eyes, because the two systems differ in many other ways and vertebrates can achieve exceptional vision.
:95. It shows instead that similar functions can be reached through different architectures whose later modifications begin from different places.
:96. Laboratory evolution lets researchers examine such sequence effects rather than merely infer them from finished bodies.
:97. Since 1988, twelve populations of Escherichia coli founded from closely related ancestral clones have been propagated by daily transfer under the same basic laboratory regime.
:98. Samples frozen at regular intervals form an archive from which earlier stages can be revived.
:99. After more than thirty thousand generations, one population in the original experiment evolved the ability to grow aerobically on citrate that was already present in its medium.
:100. The innovation did not appear as a finished leap, because an initial weak capacity was later refined into more effective use of the resource.
:101. Researchers replayed evolution from ancestors isolated at different points in that population's history.
:102. Some later genetic backgrounds produced citrate-using variants more readily than earlier ones, supporting the conclusion that prior mutations had changed the accessibility of the innovation.
:103. Those earlier changes did not dictate the final result, but they altered its probability.
:104. Other experiments have produced citrate use under stronger direct selection, so the original result does not show that only one genetic route could ever reach the trait.
:105. The twelve populations also evolved many parallel improvements, demonstrating that historical contingency operates alongside repeatable responses to common conditions.
:106. The experiment's power lies in holding both insights together: evolution is neither arbitrary nor fully predetermined.
:107. Inherited structures can open possibilities as well as close them.
:108. A feature shaped under one set of conditions may later be recruited for another function, turning yesterday's constraint into tomorrow's raw material.
:109. Human intention changes the process but does not abolish this logic.
:110. Designers can compare alternatives and coordinate large jumps, yet they still begin with existing tools, factories, skills, regulations, and users.
:111. A keyboard layout can be redesigned in an afternoon, while the trained fingers, labeled keys, shortcuts, teaching materials, and shared expectations around it cannot.
:112. The popular history of the QWERTY layout is disputed, and evidence does not justify the easy claim that it defeated a clearly superior rival merely by arriving first.
:113. Its persistence can still involve real switching costs even if its origin was not a simple accident and its alternatives are not unambiguously better.
:114. This is precisely why a sound account of path dependence cannot rest on a colorful origin story.
:115. A file format used by one organization gains value when customers, suppliers, archives, and software can read it too.
:116. That compatibility is a network effect because the value of adoption depends partly on other adopters and complementary products.
:117. Early use can also generate experience, specialized equipment, lower costs, and a larger pool of trained workers.
:118. Expectations then add another layer, because people may support the option they believe others will support.
:119. A modest lead can become self-reinforcing without any participant behaving irrationally.
:120. Rail gauges, electrical systems, paper formats, and character encodings differ across places partly because coordination occurred along different historical routes rather than around one universal engineering optimum.
:121. Once established, those standards make enormous systems interoperable.
:122. Every compatible extension strengthens the installed base and gives a future replacement more connections to rebuild.
:123. Improving a component within the existing standard may then be far cheaper than replacing the standard itself.
:124. This makes technical improvement different from system change.
:125. It also explains why a better destination on paper may not justify the cost and risk of traveling there from here.
:126. Adapters, parallel operation, backward compatibility, retraining, and staged migration can nevertheless create routes between systems that once seemed separated.
:127. Deliberate coordination can break a lock-in, although the new arrangement may begin creating its own dependencies immediately.
:128. Intention is not an escape from history but another force that can redirect it.
:129. Artificial intelligence compresses many of these dynamics into a faster-moving technical field.
:130. A successful architecture attracts software libraries, benchmark results, teaching materials, skilled workers, investment, and compatible hardware.
:131. Those complements make the architecture easier to extend and compare, which can attract still more work.
:132. Specialized processors and data centers lower the cost of some computations far more than others, making certain research questions practical and leaving others expensive.
:133. Hardware does not dictate every idea, but it changes which ideas can be tested at useful scale.
:134. Software ecosystems preserve accumulated knowledge in code, documentation, interfaces, debugging practices, and communities.
:135. Replacing one model may be quick, while replacing the surrounding workflow is not.
:136. Rapid innovation therefore does not disprove path dependence, because new systems may turn over quickly while continuing to rely on older chips, formats, metrics, and organizational routines.
:137. Speed can even deepen a path when investment arrives faster than alternatives can be explored.
:138. Training data add a second direction of influence.
:139. Records of past language, transactions, classifications, and decisions contain historical patterns rather than neutral samples from every possible world.
:140. A model trained on those records may describe or predict parts of that history, but description and prediction do not by themselves justify a normative decision.
:141. When model output helps select applicants, allocate attention, or trigger investigations, it can influence which new observations enter the next round of data.
:142. Under some conditions, this feedback can make an inherited pattern appear self-confirming.
:143. The result is not inevitable, because changed objectives, targeted data collection, audits, competing models, and human review can alter the loop.
:144. None of these measures is a universal cure, and each begins from the infrastructure already in place.
:145. Shared models and interfaces can lower entry costs and enable useful applications that smaller organizations could not build alone.
:146. Once other products and procedures rely on them, however, model changes can propagate errors, break connections, or impose expensive migration.
:147. The deepest dependencies may arise when organizations redesign roles, records, and controls around systems that perform multistep tasks.
:148. The tool adapts to the organization, the organization adapts to the tool, and each round makes separation more costly.
:149. Such systems can do more than occupy a path because they can execute further actions along it.
:150. Meanwhile, an underfunded alternative may become invisible without ever having been disproved.
:151. Historical structuring also occurs within individual lives, although not every familiar behavior deserves the label path dependence.
:152. Repetition matters when it lowers the effort of one action, weakens competing skills, reshapes the environment, or changes which alternatives a person can notice and perform.
:153. A practiced skill is a productive inheritance, not merely a limitation.
:154. Its value may justify continuing on the same path even while making retraining costly.
:155. People can also reflect on routines, seek different environments, and deliberately practice alternatives, so learned history is not a sentence imposed for life.
:156. Institutions store their past less mysteriously in statutes, files, budgets, job descriptions, professional training, and routine procedures.
:157. An institution can be understood as an answer preserved after the question that produced it has changed.
:158. Preservation is often its purpose, because courts, currencies, public records, and safety systems gain value from dependable continuity.
:159. Formal rules may be amended in a day while databases, buildings, expertise, expectations, and legal relationships take years to adjust.
:160. Administrative categories do more than describe people and events because eligibility, reporting, and enforcement are organized around them.
:161. A reform can create beneficiaries, specialists, and investments that later defend or depend on its continuation.
:162. A measure can also change behavior once rewards or penalties are attached to it, gradually separating the indicator from the quality it was meant to represent.
:163. Military systems reveal the same integration under conditions where complacency is an inadequate explanation.
:164. A weapon's usefulness depends on doctrine, training, maintenance, ammunition, communications, transport, and industrial capacity.
:165. Victory may reinforce the organizations and assumptions associated with it, even when later conditions differ.
:166. Urgency does not erase switching costs, and high stakes can make an untested transition more dangerous rather than less.
:167. None of this makes path dependence a theory of fate.
:168. An inherited starting point limits some moves and favors others without specifying a single unavoidable destination.
:169. Different histories can converge on similar functions, and similar beginnings can diverge when feedback amplifies small differences.
:170. Environmental shocks can destroy old advantages, deliberate standards can coordinate a new path, and bridges between systems can reduce transition costs.
:171. Every opening has conditions, and opening one route may close another.
:172. The costs of change also fall unevenly, so an option that looks cheap in aggregate may be prohibitive for the people or organizations required to move first.
:173. The useful question is not simply whether the current arrangement is good or bad.
:174. It is which features are load-bearing, which are replaceable, what benefits their stability supplies, and what mechanisms make alternatives costly or unseen.
:175. This avoids two symmetrical mistakes: treating the present as the necessary triumph of the best solution and treating it as a meaningless pile of historical accidents.
:176. A mature system is usually neither optimal nor arbitrary.
:177. It is a working settlement among inherited structures, present demands, accumulated investments, and unrealized alternatives.
:178. Preserving evolvability may require paying for redundancy, modularity, reversible steps, and options that never earn an immediate return.
:179. Seeing a path does not by itself open a door, but it reveals where the hinges, supports, and obstructions actually are.
:180. The past is not a verdict on the future, but it is part of the machinery by which a future becomes reachable.
