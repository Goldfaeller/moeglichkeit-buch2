== 4.2 Hardware als Schicksal ==

:1. The processors that became indispensable to modern artificial intelligence first reached a mass market by drawing faster shadows, smoother water, and more convincing explosions.
:2. In the late 1990s, graphics companies were competing over a narrow but lucrative problem: how to cover a screen with colored triangles quickly enough to sustain the illusion of a three-dimensional world.
:3. Shading a wall or fogging a corridor required similar calculations to be repeated across a great many pixels and vertices.
:4. That workload favored parallelism, which means doing many comparable pieces of arithmetic at the same time.
:5. Central processing units also contain parallel machinery, and graphics processors have never been mere crowds of identical calculators.
:6. The contrast is one of emphasis rather than kind.
:7. A central processor is designed to perform a wide variety of tasks with low delay, including complicated sequences in which each step may depend on the last.
:8. A graphics processor devotes more of its silicon to throughput across many pieces of data at once.
:9. Early graphics chips did not yet contain the thousands of general-purpose arithmetic units found in later devices, but the commercial path toward wider parallel machines had begun.
:10. Nothing about that path originally required machines to learn language, recognize objects, or generate images.
:11. It followed from the geometry of a screen, the economics of a large entertainment market, and the willingness of customers to pay for more frames per second.
:12. Two decades later, descendants of those processors occupied data centers built for scientific computing and machine learning.
:13. This was neither a scandal nor a pure coincidence.
:14. It was what happens when a research field discovers that another industry has already financed much of the instrument it needs.
:15. The fit becomes clear when one looks at what many neural networks do while they learn.
:16. Their training repeatedly multiplies arrays of numbers, adds the results, measures errors, and adjusts millions or billions of numerical parameters.
:17. The mathematics is richer than that short description suggests, because models contain different operations, data must move through memory, and later steps depend on earlier results.
:18. Yet large portions of the work can still be divided into many similar calculations that proceed in parallel.
:19. A processor built to keep many arithmetic units busy therefore has an advantage on these portions of the workload.
:20. The advantage is not automatic, because the calculations must be organized, data must arrive on time, and the program must expose enough parallel work.
:21. Hardware needs software before its theoretical speed becomes useful.
:22. During the early era of general-purpose computing on graphics processors, researchers often expressed nongraphical problems through graphics programming interfaces.
:23. They were not literally pretending every equation was a picture, but they did have to translate calculations into forms the graphics pipeline could accept.
:24. That translation demanded unusual expertise and limited which researchers could exploit the chips.
:25. Nvidia's release of CUDA in 2006 supplied a programming model and a C-based software environment for using compatible graphics processors more directly.
:26. Other programming systems and vendors also contributed to general-purpose GPU computing, but CUDA became an especially consequential bridge between hardware and research.
:27. The bridge mattered because it reduced more than execution time.
:28. It reduced the time needed to write, debug, teach, and reuse parallel programs.
:29. Libraries then packaged difficult operations so that later researchers did not have to master the hardware at the same depth.
:30. Documentation accumulated, examples circulated, and students acquired skills that employers could recognize.
:31. A chip was becoming an ecosystem.
:32. The ecosystem did not dissolve every bottleneck in machine learning, and it did not make every neural-network design efficient.
:33. It made a particular family of computational experiments cheaper, faster, and easier to repeat.
:34. That change altered which ideas could produce persuasive evidence within a research budget.
:35. The effect became visible in a famous image-recognition result in 2012.
:36. The ImageNet competition required systems to classify photographs among one thousand categories.
:37. Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton entered a deep convolutional network that sharply reduced the competition's top-five classification error.
:38. Their published system trained on about 1.2 million images for five to six days using two consumer graphics cards.
:39. The hardware did not create the result by itself.
:40. The team combined a large labeled data set, an effective model, careful training choices, and code engineered around the memory limits of the two devices.
:41. Nor did the result spring from mathematical ideas invented in a single year.
:42. Convolutional networks, multilayer neural models, and gradient-based learning had long research histories, and practical systems had achieved successes before 2012.
:43. What the graphics processors changed was the scale at which this combination could be tested in an ordinary research setting.
:44. An approach that had seemed costly and limited could now produce a result conspicuous enough to redirect attention and resources.
:45. The shift should not be compressed into the claim that neural networks had simply been abandoned until gamers rescued them.
:46. Research activity had risen and fallen unevenly across countries, institutions, and applications, while algorithms, data sets, and processors all continued to develop.
:47. Some earlier disappointments reflected genuine technical limits, some reflected inflated expectations, and some reflected experiments that were too expensive to run at useful scale.
:48. Those causes matter because they imply different lessons.
:49. A refuted claim has failed an evidential test, whereas an unaffordable experiment may never have received one.
:50. Scientific memory often records both outcomes with the same quiet absence from the next generation of papers.
:51. The video-game market did not select the best theory of intelligence.
:52. It mass-produced a tool that made certain theories of machine learning unusually testable.
:53. Once those theories yielded strong results, demand from machine learning began reshaping the tool in return.
:54. This sequence is an instance of what computer scientist Sara Hooker called the hardware lottery.
:55. The phrase does not mean that research success is random or that technical merit is irrelevant.
:56. It names a match between an idea and the hardware and software available when that idea must prove itself.
:57. An approach can be sound yet arrive when its experiments are prohibitively expensive.
:58. Another can prosper because its operations map neatly onto equipment already manufactured at scale.
:59. The first approach has not thereby been proved inferior, and the second has not thereby been proved optimal.
:60. The available substrate has changed the cost of the comparison.
:61. Cost influences how many trials can be run, how quickly errors can be corrected, and how many researchers can participate.
:62. It therefore changes the probability that an approach will improve enough to win the next comparison as well.
:63. This is where a historical circumstance becomes path dependence rather than mere background.
:64. Existing hardware lowers the cost of one route, success attracts tools and talent, and those complements lower its future cost again.
:65. A one-time advantage becomes a self-reinforcing sequence.
:66. The history of computing contains earlier mismatches between an idea and its physical substrate, although none is a perfect copy of the GPU story.
:67. In 1834, Charles Babbage began designing the Analytical Engine, a programmable mechanical computer with a separate store for numbers and a mill for calculation.
:68. His plans incorporated punched-card instructions, loops, and conditional branching, features that later readers can recognize without pretending that the machine was a Victorian laptop.
:69. The Analytical Engine was never completed.
:70. Its failure cannot be assigned simply to bad logic or simply to workshops incapable of precision.
:71. His earlier Difference Engine project had already consumed public funds and ended in dispute, while the government declined to finance the newer Analytical Engine.
:72. Victorian manufacturing could produce impressive precision work, but building and coordinating thousands of mechanical parts at the required cost and reliability posed a formidable systems problem.
:73. Electronic switches later made general-purpose computation practical on a different physical basis.
:74. That later success vindicated important features of Babbage's logical design without showing that his proposed machine could have been built economically in his own setting.
:75. The case distinguishes physical possibility from historical accessibility.
:76. A machine may be conceivable and even mechanically possible while remaining outside the affordable neighborhood of its time.
:77. Specialized artificial-intelligence computers of the 1980s offer a different caution.
:78. Lisp machines combined hardware and software designed for the programming language then widely used in artificial-intelligence research.
:79. They offered development environments that users valued and that generic machines initially struggled to match.
:80. Their commercial decline had several causes, including high prices, a limited market, changes in artificial-intelligence funding, improving commodity workstations, and Lisp implementations that migrated to more general hardware.
:81. It would be too simple to say that specialized machines were technically outclassed in a fair contest.
:82. Commodity systems drew on larger production volumes and broader software markets, while specialized vendors had to sustain an entire platform with fewer customers.
:83. Scale changed the contest while the contest was underway.
:84. The lesson is not that specialized hardware must lose, because later accelerators provide obvious counterexamples.
:85. The lesson is that a good match between a machine and a task must survive the economics of the surrounding ecosystem.
:86. Hardware first affects which ideas can be tested, then reaches deeper into the form those ideas take.
:87. Researchers work under deadlines, budgets, and expectations of publishable results.
:88. They naturally prefer experiments that can finish while the result still matters.
:89. When one calculation is far cheaper than another, the difference does not merely accelerate an answer.
:90. It changes which questions appear feasible enough to ask.
:91. Dense matrix operations suit modern accelerators because they expose regular parallel work and can reuse data efficiently.
:92. Irregular computations, frequent branching, and scattered memory access can leave the same hardware underused.
:93. These are tendencies rather than prohibitions, since compilers, specialized kernels, and newer devices can handle some irregular work well.
:94. Nevertheless, a method that fights the memory system or leaves arithmetic units idle begins each experiment with a cost penalty.
:95. Grant proposals, conference deadlines, and product schedules turn that engineering penalty into a selection pressure on ideas.
:96. A research group may simplify an algorithm, batch examples together, or choose model dimensions that fit efficient hardware operations.
:97. Such adaptations are usually sensible responses to real constraints.
:98. Collectively, they mean that models carry traces of the machines on which they were developed.
:99. The relation resembles the mark that available stone leaves on an old building, although software is more revisable than masonry and computing hardware can be redesigned deliberately.
:100. The analogy reveals inheritance, not inevitability.
:101. Biological nervous systems make the contrast vivid but do not settle it.
:102. Neurons communicate sparsely and in events, whereas much mainstream machine learning is organized around dense numerical arrays processed in synchronized batches.
:103. A brain and a graphics processor differ in materials, architecture, learning process, and function, so energy comparisons between them cannot identify a winner by themselves.
:104. Even so, the contrast has motivated serious work on neuromorphic processors that integrate memory and computation more closely and respond to sparse events.
:105. Research chips have demonstrated advantages on selected sensing, control, optimization, and spiking-network workloads.
:106. They have not established that a brain-inspired architecture is a general replacement for graphics processors or other accelerators.
:107. Results depend on the task, the model, the surrounding software, and what is included in the energy and latency accounting.
:108. Their relative immaturity is also part of the comparison.
:109. A new processor begins without decades of libraries, debugging tools, trained engineers, production experience, and compatible data-center equipment.
:110. Weak early results may reveal a poor architecture, an immature ecosystem, or both.
:111. Distinguishing those explanations requires investment in the very ecosystem whose value remains uncertain.
:112. This is the hardware lottery's hardest loop.
:113. Evidence is needed before an alternative attracts resources, but resources are needed before the alternative can produce fair evidence.
:114. Domain-specific accelerators also show that the loop can open.
:115. A tensor processing unit deployed in data centers in the mid-2010s used dedicated matrix-multiplication hardware to improve the cost and energy performance of neural-network inference.
:116. This was a significant departure from relying only on central and graphics processors.
:117. Yet it largely accelerated workloads already expressed through established neural-network software and numerical operations.
:118. The new branch changed the hardware path while inheriting much of the computational path above it.
:119. Path changes are often hybrids of novelty and compatibility rather than clean escapes.
:120. Once a workload becomes commercially important, chip designers optimize directly for it.
:121. They add lower-precision arithmetic, faster matrix units, larger or differently arranged memories, and links for connecting many processors.
:122. Software developers then tune libraries to those features.
:123. Model designers see that some operations have become cheaper and use more of them.
:124. Customers generate demand for the next chip based on models designed for the previous one.
:125. The loop has closed.
:126. Each turn can improve performance, reduce cost, and make previously impossible experiments routine.
:127. Those are real benefits rather than symptoms of a mistake.
:128. The same improvement raises the relative price of approaches that cannot use the optimized stack.
:129. An alternative need not deteriorate to fall behind.
:130. It can remain unchanged while processors, libraries, benchmarks, and expertise advance around its rival.
:131. After several cycles, the resulting performance gap looks like a verdict on the idea even when it partly records unequal investment.
:132. This does not imply that every neglected idea deserves revival.
:133. Most untested architectures may be impractical, inefficient, or unnecessary.
:134. The difficulty is that neglect supplies little evidence about which failures are fundamental and which are temporary.
:135. The physical production of advanced chips strengthens this asymmetry.
:136. Leading-edge fabrication plants require investments measured in many billions of dollars, years of planning, and tightly coordinated supplies of lithography equipment, optics, chemicals, materials, packaging, and power.
:137. Many links in that chain demand knowledge and facilities that cannot be reproduced quickly by a new entrant.
:138. A processor architecture is therefore embedded in factories, design tools, supplier contracts, testing procedures, and data centers before a researcher writes a line of model code.
:139. Capital already committed to those complements becomes a reason to improve the established path rather than replace it.
:140. These are sunk costs, but the stronger binding force is often prospective.
:141. Switching would require new software, retrained staff, revised facilities, uncertain supply, and a period in which the replacement may perform worse.
:142. The expected future transition cost, not reverence for past spending, narrows the practical choice.
:143. Supply concentration adds vulnerability as well as efficiency.
:144. Specialization lets firms solve extraordinarily difficult manufacturing problems, but disruption at a scarce link can delay every downstream plan that assumes its availability.
:145. Hardware thus shapes research through both abundance and scarcity.
:146. Mass-produced accelerators make vast experiments possible, while access to the largest clusters remains uneven.
:147. Empirical studies of the compute divide have found a pronounced gap between the resources used by leading industrial laboratories and those available to many academic teams.
:148. That gap is especially important for work whose central claim depends on training or repeatedly evaluating very large models.
:149. Researchers without comparable resources can still contribute algorithms, theory, audits, smaller-scale experiments, data work, and critical analysis.
:150. Access to pretrained models can lower some barriers while creating dependence on systems whose construction outsiders could not observe or reproduce.
:151. A result that is too expensive to rerun is not automatically false.
:152. It is harder to scrutinize, vary, and refute independently, which weakens one route by which science corrects itself.
:153. Shared computing facilities, structured access programs, open artifacts, and more efficient experimental designs can provide counterweights.
:154. None removes the underlying cost, and each allocates scarce access by some rule.
:155. Astronomy offers a useful comparison because researchers have long built institutions around instruments no single laboratory could own.
:156. The comparison has limits, since a telescope observes a shared sky while a machine-learning cluster may depend on proprietary data, code, and models.
:157. Still, both fields show that scarce instruments do not dictate a single social arrangement for verification.
:158. Institutions can widen or narrow access to the same physical machinery.
:159. Time creates one final asymmetry among the layers of the system.
:160. A paper can be revised quickly, software more slowly, a processor design more slowly still, and a fabrication plant or power connection on a longer schedule again.
:161. The most material choices are often the least reversible within a research generation.
:162. A decision in a chip roadmap can propagate upward into libraries, model architectures, hiring, and published comparisons for years.
:163. By the time the physical layer could be replaced, many faster-moving layers have learned to depend on it.
:164. This is lock-in, but it is not complete immobility.
:165. New accelerators can enter through compatibility layers, specialized niches, public investment, or demand from an unrelated market.
:166. Software can hide some hardware differences, and efficient algorithms can make formerly expensive questions affordable.
:167. A shock in energy prices, supply, regulation, or application demand can also change which characteristics matter.
:168. The title's word “destiny” is therefore a deliberate exaggeration.
:169. Hardware does not dictate a theory's truth, forbid all alternatives, or guarantee that today's dominant design will remain dominant.
:170. It sets prices, delays, performance ceilings, and compatibility requirements that accumulate across repeated choices.
:171. Applied once, such a constraint is merely a condition.
:172. Applied through feedback among chips, software, skills, investment, and expectations, it reshapes the reachable possibilities of the field.
:173. The established path can be extraordinarily productive and constraining at the same time.
:174. Its productivity explains why people continue to deepen it, and that deepening explains why leaving becomes expensive.
:175. Somewhere among the possible machines are architectures that would serve no useful purpose.
:176. Others may answer questions that today's equipment makes awkward to formulate, let alone test.
:177. Argument alone cannot reliably separate the two groups, because the decisive evidence requires working machines and mature tools.
:178. The last major opening came from an industry pursuing a different goal, when graphics hardware built for virtual worlds became an instrument for machine learning.
:179. The next opening may likewise arrive through deliberate research, an adjacent market, or a problem that makes a neglected property valuable.
:180. The explosions on the old screen did not determine the destination, but they helped pay for the road on which much of artificial intelligence now travels.
