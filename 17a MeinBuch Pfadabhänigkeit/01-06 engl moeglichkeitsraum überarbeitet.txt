== 1.6 Rückkopplung und Selbstverstärkung ==

:1. Many of our intuitions about systems begin with something that pushes back.
:2. Heat an object and it sheds energy into cooler surroundings; stretch a spring within its working range and it pulls toward its earlier shape.
:3. A thermostat makes the principle visible by turning the heat off when a room grows too warm and on again when the temperature falls.
:4. This is negative feedback: a departure from a target or stable condition triggers effects that tend to reduce the departure.
:5. Negative feedback can stabilize a system, although delays, excessive responses, or changing conditions can still produce oscillation or failure.
:6. It is not a guarantee of calm, but it helps explain why many disturbances fade instead of compounding.
:7. Then there is the other kind, and the other kind is where history can accumulate.
:8. Positive feedback means that an initial change alters conditions in ways that make further change in the same direction more likely.
:9. A crowd can attract more onlookers because a crowd is already there.
:10. A rumor can find new carriers because earlier transmission has made it more visible.
:11. A language becomes more useful to a learner when more potential partners already speak it.
:12. Positive in this phrase does not mean beneficial, just as negative does not mean harmful.
:13. The signs describe the direction of the response, not its moral value.
:14. Nor does positive feedback mean that nothing ever pushes back.
:15. It means that, over some range and for some period, reinforcement is stronger than the forces opposing it.
:16. That qualification matters because real crowds disperse, rumors exhaust their audience, and networks encounter costs, rivals, and capacity limits.
:17. The consequences can nevertheless be large because reinforcement gives a system a way to retain and magnify an early difference.
:18. Without such a mechanism, history may occur without becoming path dependence.
:19. Imagine that a coin toss determines who speaks first at a meeting, but the order confers no information, authority, or later advantage.
:20. The toss belongs to the meeting's history, yet it does not change the later set of options in any important way.
:21. Now suppose that the first speaker's proposal becomes the draft on the screen, attracts the first comments, and defines the terms in which alternatives must be expressed.
:22. The same small event has begun to reorganize what later participants can easily see, amend, or reject.
:23. Positive feedback can therefore be the machinery that converts contingency into structure.
:24. The contingent event supplies a difference, while the loop preserves and enlarges it.
:25. Neither part is sufficient by itself.
:26. A random advantage that is not reinforced can disappear among the next thousand events.
:27. A powerful reinforcing process with no competing alternatives may create growth or stability without making the outcome contingent on an early fork.
:28. Path dependence arises when sequence matters because earlier steps change the benefits, costs, visibility, or feasibility of later steps.
:29. This definition is narrower than the claim that the past influences the present, which is true of nearly everything.
:30. It also avoids the opposite exaggeration that every amplified beginning must end in permanent lock-in.
:31. Economists often analyze one important family of these processes under the heading of increasing returns to adoption.
:32. The phrase does not simply mean that every additional unit of effort produces more output than the one before it.
:33. It means that adopting an option can create conditions that make further adoption of the same or a compatible option more attractive.
:34. Those conditions can arise through network effects, learning, economies of scale, complementary investments, or expectations.
:35. The mechanisms can reinforce one another, but they should not be treated as synonyms.
:36. Diminishing returns can also operate in the same system, because a network may become more valuable to users even as congestion makes each additional connection harder to serve.
:37. The balance among these forces determines whether growth accelerates, levels off, fragments, or reverses.
:38. Consider the clearest case, which is a communication network.
:39. A telephone connected to nobody is of little use as a telephone.
:40. Connected to one other telephone, it can reach one party, and connected to a large interoperable network, it can reach many.
:41. Part of the instrument's value therefore depends not on the instrument alone but on the number of compatible people and services it can reach.
:42. This is a direct network effect because another participant directly enlarges the opportunities available to existing participants.
:43. The effect belongs to the compatible network, not necessarily to one company, device, or brand.
:44. Two providers that interconnect may contribute to the same useful network even while competing with each other.
:45. Compatibility can thus weaken lock-in by allowing users to switch suppliers without abandoning everyone on the other side of the connection.
:46. An indirect network effect works differently.
:47. A widely used device or format can attract repair services, training, accessories, software, and other complementary products.
:48. Users value the larger ecosystem, while producers of complements prefer the larger pool of customers.
:49. Each side can make the other more attractive without any single additional user directly benefiting every other one.
:50. Expectations add another loop because people often choose a standard partly on the belief that it will remain supported.
:51. Their choice then helps create the installed base that makes the expectation more plausible.
:52. A forecast can become one cause of its own fulfillment, though price, performance, coordination, and strategic behavior still matter.
:53. This mechanism helps explain why timing can matter so much in contests among incompatible systems.
:54. An early lead can attract complements and confidence, which widen the lead and make later comparison less neutral.
:55. The dominant option does not have to be the technically best along every dimension.
:56. It may prevail because it is good enough and already connected to more users, skills, and investments.
:57. Yet it would be equally wrong to conclude that superiority is irrelevant or that every losing technology was secretly better.
:58. Quality can create the early lead, preserve it, or allow a challenger to overcome an incumbent's network advantage.
:59. Price cuts, interoperability, sponsorship, regulation, or a sufficiently large improvement can also reopen a contest that looked settled.
:60. Network effects alter the comparison; they do not abolish comparison.
:61. Learning is another form of reinforcement, and it operates through experience rather than connection.
:62. The more often people perform a task with a technology, the more opportunities they have to discover shortcuts, correct errors, redesign tools, and organize production around it.
:63. Later units can consequently require less labor or fewer resources than earlier ones.
:64. This is real improvement, not an illusion produced by familiarity.
:65. It also has a history, because the improvement accrues disproportionately to the option that received the practice.
:66. An initially promising rival may then be compared in its immature state with an incumbent refined by years of use.
:67. The comparison records current performance accurately while concealing the unequal amounts of learning embodied in the two options.
:68. Skills reinforce the same asymmetry.
:69. Workers train on the tools employers already use, and employers choose tools for which trained workers can be hired.
:70. Neither side needs to admire the standard for the loop to continue.
:71. Their investments are sensible responses to one another, and together those responses make departure more costly.
:72. Economies of scale create a related but distinct advantage.
:73. When fixed costs can be spread across more units, an established design may become cheaper to produce or maintain.
:74. High volume can support specialized suppliers and dedicated equipment that would not be economical for a small rival.
:75. Lower costs can draw more buyers, and more buyers can justify another round of cost reduction.
:76. Scale is not limitless, however, because congestion, coordination burdens, resource scarcity, or managerial complexity may eventually raise costs.
:77. The important claim is not that reinforcement runs forever but that it can reshape the terrain before counterforces catch it.
:78. Complementary investment supplies another layer.
:79. Once organizations build databases, training programs, contracts, buildings, or procedures around a standard, the standard becomes one component of a larger working arrangement.
:80. Replacing it may require changing several connected components at once.
:81. The original purchase price can be small compared with the future cost of coordinating that transition.
:82. These prospective switching costs matter more than money already spent merely because past expenditure feels painful to abandon.
:83. Sunk costs are gone under either choice, whereas compatible assets and acquired skills change what a future choice will actually cost.
:84. A path binds when the past has been built into the requirements of the next step.
:85. That is why an ecosystem can be harder to replace than any object within it.
:86. Reputation and visibility can produce cumulative advantage in social systems.
:87. Attention tends to flow toward work that has already received attention, because earlier recognition makes later recognition easier.
:88. Sociologist Robert Merton called one version of this pattern the Matthew effect when analyzing how credit and visibility accumulate in science.
:89. The name came from a biblical line about more being given to those who already have.
:90. A recognized researcher may be read sooner, invited more often, and trusted with more resources, creating further opportunities for recognized work.
:91. This does not show that merit is absent at every stage.
:92. Strong work may generate the first recognition, and later judgments may still respond to quality.
:93. The feedback claim is more precise: equal contributions need not receive equal attention once their authors occupy unequal positions in the network of recognition.
:94. Small differences can widen when each reward improves access to the next reward.
:95. They do not have to widen without bound, because careers end, fields change, evaluation rules shift, and neglected work can be rediscovered.
:96. Cumulative advantage is therefore a mechanism for inequality, not a universal proof that every unequal outcome began in chance or injustice.
:97. The distinction guards against two symmetrical errors.
:98. One error treats success as conclusive evidence of intrinsic superiority.
:99. The other treats success as nothing but luck amplified by a blind system.
:100. Feedback analysis asks instead which advantages were earned, inherited, constructed, or mutually reinforced, and how each one altered the next choice.
:101. The connection between reinforcement and lock-in is direct.
:102. Lock-in describes a condition in which leaving a path has become costly enough that users remain even when another option has attractive features.
:103. Feedback describes one way that condition develops.
:104. Each turn of the loop can add users, competence, complementary assets, expectations, or rules.
:105. Each addition can make the next adoption more attractive and the next departure more disruptive.
:106. The window in which alternatives can compete on roughly equal terms may therefore close before anyone formally decides to close it.
:107. It closes not because technical change becomes impossible but because the comparison now includes the cost of moving an interdependent system.
:108. This is a hard constraint when physical infrastructure must be rebuilt and a softer one when habits or expectations can change quickly.
:109. Most real lock-ins combine several kinds of cost and are matters of degree rather than absolute imprisonment.
:110. Even a strong lock-in can be broken by shocks, coordinated migration, adapters, subsidies, new interfaces, or a challenger whose advantages exceed the transition cost.
:111. The change may also proceed gradually as old and new components coexist during a bridge period.
:112. Such routes matter because path dependence is not determinism.
:113. Earlier choices modify the price and probability of later choices without dictating a single future.
:114. Reinforcement can also create enormous benefits.
:115. A shared safety code is valuable partly because designers, inspectors, manufacturers, and emergency services can rely on the same expectations.
:116. A common language or payment standard reduces the work required for coordination.
:117. Stable institutions can support trust because one successful exchange makes another exchange less risky.
:118. Knowledge builds on knowledge because established methods, archives, and terminology allow later investigators to begin beyond the starting point of their predecessors.
:119. These are useful bindings, not unfortunate leftovers waiting to be swept away.
:120. Their benefit is precisely what makes them costly to abandon when conditions change.
:121. The loop is indifferent to whether people approve of what it amplifies.
:122. The same structure can compound skill or exclusion, compatibility or dependence, trust or corruption.
:123. Evaluation requires asking what the loop produces, for whom, and at what cost, but causal explanation begins by identifying the loop itself.
:124. A deeper form of feedback appears when a system changes the environment to which it must later respond.
:125. A beaver builds a dam, slowing water and transforming part of a stream into pond and wetland habitat.
:126. The altered setting affects the beaver and many other organisms that encounter it.
:127. Descendants may inhabit environmental conditions partly produced by earlier generations rather than conditions untouched by them.
:128. Biologists call this process niche construction when organisms modify features of their environment in ways that can alter development, ecology, or selection.
:129. Earthworms offer another example because their burrowing and processing of material change the structure and chemistry of soil.
:130. Some of those changes persist beyond the lives of individual worms and become part of the environment encountered by later generations.
:131. Researchers use the term ecological inheritance for such inherited environmental legacies, although it is not genetic inheritance and need not pass through a strict parent-to-offspring line.
:132. Niche construction complicates the picture of an environment acting on organisms from only one direction.
:133. Organisms modify conditions, modified conditions affect survival and reproduction, and later organisms modify those conditions again.
:134. The causal arrows form a loop, but that does not make every effect equally strong or erase the importance of external climate, predators, resources, and chance.
:135. Nor is every alteration of an environment automatically a major evolutionary cause.
:136. Biologists agree that organisms modify environments, while they continue to debate how much explanatory weight niche construction should carry as a distinct evolutionary process.
:137. The careful claim is powerful enough: a population can inherit not only genes but also a partly altered field of pressures and opportunities.
:138. Human-built systems make an analogous pattern especially visible, although cultural institutions are not biological organisms.
:139. A technology can create infrastructure that makes further development along its own architecture cheaper and alternatives less compatible.
:140. An institution can create offices, records, constituencies, and legal categories that make some reforms easier to formulate than others.
:141. A military doctrine can guide equipment purchases, and the resulting equipment can make some later doctrines practical and others prohibitively expensive.
:142. In each case, earlier activity helps build the environment in which later choices are evaluated.
:143. The system need not fit that environment perfectly, and the environment is never entirely of its own making.
:144. It may simply fit well enough that local correction favors improvement within the path over movement to another one.
:145. This is one reason a historically contingent arrangement can appear natural when viewed only from inside its supporting infrastructure.
:146. Feedback loops also need not proceed smoothly, but sudden change should not be assumed whenever reinforcement is present.
:147. Some loops grow gradually, some saturate, and some remain weak because opposing feedback cancels their effect.
:148. Others cross a threshold after accumulated change makes a new behavior possible, causing the visible transition to occur faster than the buildup behind it.
:149. The threshold is a feature to demonstrate in a particular system, not a universal story to impose on revolutions, markets, or ecosystems.
:150. A serious analysis therefore looks for the stock that accumulates, the link that returns an effect to its cause, the delays in the loop, and the forces that limit it.
:151. It also asks whether an early difference truly changed later possibilities or merely happened before them.
:152. Positive feedback turns into durable path dependence only when reinforcement lasts long enough to build assets, expectations, skills, interfaces, or environmental legacies that survive the original event.
:153. By then the path may be useful, burdensome, or both, and leaving it may remain possible even when leaving it is no longer simple.
:154. The deepest mark of self-reinforcement is therefore not endless acceleration but a changed neighborhood of reachable next steps.
:155. What began as a small deviation has become part of the ground from which every later movement must start.
