== 4.1 Die Architektur, die alles bestimmte ==

:1. There is a diagram that almost anyone entering modern machine learning will encounter early.
:2. It is not much to look at.
:3. Two columns of rectangles stand on top of one another, arrows run upward, a few loops return to the side, and half a dozen labels sit in small type.
:4. First published in 2017, versions of that diagram now appear in papers, lectures, software documentation, and classrooms around the world.
:5. It describes an arrangement of mathematical operations for processing sequences, and the arrangement is called a transformer.
:6. Transformer components now sit inside many influential systems for language, images, audio, and scientific data.
:7. They often appear in modified or hybrid form, which already complicates the idea that one unchanging architecture conquered every domain.
:8. Convolutional networks, recurrent models, graph networks, state-space models, and systems built around explicit rules have not vanished.
:9. The title of this chapter is therefore a deliberate exaggeration.
:10. The transformer did not determine everything, but it changed which experiments became easy to run, which results became easy to compare, and which research programs became easy to finance.
:11. That difference matters because an architecture does more than map inputs to outputs.
:12. Once widely adopted, it gathers software, hardware, trained specialists, benchmarks, and investment around itself.
:13. Those complements improve the architecture while also raising the cost of departing from it.
:14. There are two tempting explanations for its spread, and the tension between them reveals why technical success and historical accumulation are so difficult to separate.
:15. The first is that the arrangement captures useful and unusually general relationships in structured data.
:16. The second is that it became dominant because more and more people learned how to build, run, measure, and pay for it.
:17. Neither explanation can be dismissed, and the most defensible account includes both.
:18. Technical merit helped create the path, while the path helped magnify the returns to technical merit.
:19. Before 2017, many leading systems for translating or otherwise transforming sequences relied on recurrent or convolutional neural networks.
:20. A recurrent network processes a sequence by carrying a numerical state from one position to the next.
:21. Each update depends on the state produced at the preceding step, so the steps along a sequence cannot all be computed simultaneously during training.
:22. The model may perform many operations within a step and process many examples at once, but the chain of states still creates a serial dependency.
:23. Long short-term memory networks, introduced by Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber in 1997, used gates and a protected error path to make long-range learning more tractable.
:24. Later variants became important in translation, speech recognition, language modeling, and other sequence tasks.
:25. They worked well, and their success created its own stock of code, expertise, and compatible methods.
:26. Their serial structure nevertheless limited how fully training could exploit machines designed for parallel arithmetic.
:27. That limitation did not arise from language itself.
:28. It arose from the chosen computational representation of language.
:29. At the same time, graphics processors were becoming central to machine learning.
:30. Developed for highly parallel image calculations and increasingly used for general-purpose computing, these processors were especially effective at large blocks of matrix arithmetic.
:31. They could perform many similar operations together, provided the computation exposed enough independent work.
:32. Recurrent models and parallel processors were not incompatible, but their strengths did not line up perfectly.
:33. The mismatch was one important constraint on training speed, though hardly the only obstacle facing the field.
:34. In 2017 eight researchers at Google published a paper titled “Attention Is All You Need.”
:35. Attention mechanisms already allowed a model to assign different weights to different parts of an input rather than compressing everything into one running summary.
:36. The new architecture moved attention to the center and removed recurrence and convolution from its main sequence-processing blocks.
:37. In a transformer encoder, representations of all input positions can be processed together.
:38. Each position is projected into numerical objects conventionally called a query, a key, and a value.
:39. The compatibility between queries and keys produces weights that determine how values are combined.
:40. Several attention heads can learn different patterns of relationship in parallel.
:41. Because attention by itself does not know whether a token came first or last, information about position must be supplied separately.
:42. The original paper used positional encodings added to the input representations.
:43. This design made training much more parallelizable than training a recurrent network along the sequence dimension.
:44. It did not abolish sequence.
:45. Autoregressive transformer decoders still generate one token after another at inference time, unless a different generation scheme is used.
:46. Nor did the design make computation free.
:47. Full self-attention forms interactions among every pair of positions, so its time and memory demands grow quadratically with sequence length in the standard formulation.
:48. Doubling a long input can therefore make the attention calculation roughly four times as large.
:49. That is a serious disadvantage for long sequences, but it is not proof that the whole design is theoretically worse.
:50. Direct global interaction between positions can shorten the path through which distant information must travel inside a network.
:51. The architecture traded one kind of difficulty for another, and the new trade fit the available accelerators unusually well.
:52. The original paper reported strong results on two machine-translation tasks and emphasized greater parallelism and substantially shorter training time than the recurrent and convolutional systems it compared against.
:53. It also tested the model on English constituency parsing, but it did not claim to have found a universal blueprint for intelligence.
:54. The paper was a focused technical contribution, not a declaration that an entire discipline had reached its final form.
:55. What followed depended on more than the paper alone.
:56. Researchers adapted transformer ideas to language pretraining, vision, speech, protein structure, and other domains, often changing the architecture to match the problem.
:57. Some adaptations succeeded because attention could flexibly connect distant or otherwise related elements.
:58. Others succeeded because the same computational primitives could be reused across large data sets and powerful accelerators.
:59. These are related advantages, but they are not the same advantage.
:60. One concerns what the model can represent, while the other concerns how efficiently an institution can develop and operate it.
:61. Empirical studies of transformer language models then found regular scaling relationships under specified training conditions.
:62. Average prediction loss often fell approximately as a power law when researchers increased model size, data, and computation in coordinated ways.
:63. These scaling laws describe measured regimes, not laws of nature and not guarantees that every desirable capability improves smoothly.
:64. They also involve diminishing returns, data quality, and choices about how a fixed computing budget is divided between parameters and training examples.
:65. More spending did not automatically buy wisdom, reliability, or suitability for every task.
:66. It did make improvement on important training and evaluation measures predictable enough to support large investments.
:67. The architecture consequently became more than a research choice.
:68. It became a platform around which laboratories and companies could organize long projects.
:69. Software libraries implemented transformer blocks, and later versions made those blocks faster, easier to distribute, and less expensive to run.
:70. Compiler teams and kernel specialists tuned the innermost arithmetic to particular accelerators and memory systems.
:71. Chip designers, meanwhile, improved matrix engines, numerical formats, memory bandwidth, and interconnects for a broad range of machine-learning workloads that included transformers.
:72. It would be too simple to say that the transformer created all of this hardware, since specialized matrix hardware and deep-learning accelerators were already developing alongside it.
:73. The more precise claim is reciprocal.
:74. The architecture benefited from an existing trajectory in parallel computation, and its growing workloads gave hardware and software developers stronger reasons to optimize that trajectory further.
:75. Each turn of this loop improved the pairing.
:76. Success attracted complementary investment, and complementary investment increased the probability of further success.
:77. The arrangement also entered the curriculum.
:78. It is taught in courses, explained in tutorials, implemented in exercises, and assumed by many hiring processes.
:79. Practitioners acquire intuitions about memory limits, optimization failures, and useful modifications by working repeatedly with the same family of designs.
:80. Those skills are real productive assets, not mere habits.
:81. Large pretrained models and reusable checkpoints further lower the cost of beginning a project within the established family.
:82. A researcher can adapt an existing model instead of assembling every capability from the beginning.
:83. Common benchmarks and reporting conventions make results easier to compare across laboratories.
:84. Shared tools, trained people, reusable models, and common measures together make cumulative research possible at extraordinary speed.
:85. This is the useful side of lock-in.
:86. Stability prevents every project from paying the full cost of reinvention.
:87. The same stability creates switching costs because an alternative must replace not only a model but also some portion of the surrounding system.
:88. That is where ordinary continuity becomes path dependence.
:89. The architecture has a history merely because it was invented in the past.
:90. It becomes path-dependent when the sequence of adoption and investment changes the cost, visibility, or probability of later research choices.
:91. Early performance attracts users, users justify tooling, tooling improves performance, and improved performance attracts more users.
:92. The loop does not make alternatives physically impossible.
:93. It makes some alternatives cheaper to test and others more expensive even to formulate in comparable terms.
:94. The clearest way to see the effect is to imagine proposing a fundamentally different architecture.
:95. Suppose the idea is genuinely promising, although no one can know in advance whether it is better overall.
:96. To establish its value, you must build it and compare it with a strong incumbent.
:97. The incumbent arrives with mature libraries, optimized kernels, established training procedures, trained models, diagnostic tools, and baselines familiar to reviewers.
:98. Your first implementation may arrive with a small team, limited computing time, and bugs that the incumbent's community eliminated years ago.
:99. You are not yet comparing two equally developed ideas.
:100. You are comparing an idea with an ecosystem.
:101. This does not guarantee that the challenger will lose.
:102. It does mean that early experimental results mix architectural quality with unequal engineering maturity.
:103. A weak result can therefore show that an approach is weak, that its implementation is immature, that its training recipe is unsuitable, or some combination of the three.
:104. Reviewers who demand competitive baselines are not behaving irrationally.
:105. Their standards protect the literature from claims supported only by easy comparisons.
:106. A laboratory director who declines an uncertain multiyear project may be protecting students whose careers depend on timely results.
:107. A graduate student who chooses a modification of a proven model over a radical replacement may be responding sensibly to scarce time and computing access.
:108. Funders may prefer a project whose milestones can be stated in the field's established units.
:109. Every individual decision can be defensible while their aggregate narrows the region of the possibility space that receives sustained exploration.
:110. This is not evidence that the community has ceased exploring.
:111. Researchers continue to develop recurrent networks, convolutions, graph methods, explicit reasoning systems, state-space models, and mixtures of several approaches.
:112. Their existence is an important counterexample to any claim of complete lock-in.
:113. The way some alternatives become competitive nevertheless reveals the inherited constraints.
:114. Structured state-space models, for example, revive the idea of carrying a state through a sequence while reformulating the computation for efficient training on parallel hardware.
:115. The S4 architecture showed that a carefully structured state-space system could be evaluated efficiently as a convolution and used recurrently for generation.
:116. Later selective state-space designs introduced input-dependent updates and hardware-aware parallel algorithms.
:117. These systems are not simple returns to the recurrent networks that transformers displaced.
:118. They are new constructions designed in a world whose accelerators, software stacks, data pipelines, and benchmarks were heavily shaped by deep learning at scale.
:119. Even an escape route can pass through part of the constraint.
:120. Alternatives that reuse tensor libraries, automatic differentiation, accelerator clusters, standard data sets, and familiar evaluation procedures face lower entry costs.
:121. Hybrid models lower those costs further by replacing only selected components rather than the whole stack.
:122. Such changes can be technically substantial even when the resulting system retains the transformer label.
:123. The label therefore hides variation as well as enforcing continuity.
:124. Modular blocks allow attention, recurrence, convolution, routing, or external memory to be combined without discarding every complementary investment.
:125. This capacity for internal modification makes the established family more evolvable than a rigid standard would be.
:126. It also directs innovation toward changes that fit the established interfaces.
:127. Approaches requiring different hardware, different data representations, different training objectives, and different measures must cross several barriers at once.
:128. The surrounding apparatus reaches into evaluation itself.
:129. Many optimization methods, initialization practices, numerical formats, and debugging techniques have been refined alongside transformer systems.
:130. Benchmarks used by the field often test tasks on which transformer baselines are already extensively tuned.
:131. A challenger assessed with those instruments may face a meaningful test, but not a neutral test of architecture alone.
:132. A fair comparison would require comparable data, computing budgets, engineering effort, and opportunities to learn from failure.
:133. Even equal raw computation would not erase the incumbent's accumulated knowledge.
:134. Changing the benchmark could reduce one bias while introducing another, because no measure captures every use or every trade-off.
:135. Rigorous comparison is indispensable, yet the conventions that make comparison rigorous can still embody a historical path.
:136. That is why the constraint is easy to miss.
:137. It often feels like ordinary competence.
:138. The strongest rival explanation remains straightforward: perhaps transformers dominate because, for many important tasks, they really are the best architecture so far developed.
:139. Their empirical record gives that explanation substantial weight.
:140. Path dependence does not deny technical merit, and dominance is not evidence of inferiority.
:141. It asks a different causal question about how merit and accumulated advantage interact.
:142. A strong design can become stronger through learning effects, scale, compatibility, and stable expectations.
:143. Once that happens, observed dominance no longer measures the original design in isolation.
:144. It measures the design plus the history built around it.
:145. The romantic opposite is equally mistaken.
:146. An underfunded alternative is not secretly superior merely because it lost, and keeping every possible approach alive would consume money, talent, and time that could be used elsewhere.
:147. The evidential problem is narrower: uneven development leaves some comparisons unresolved.
:148. Biologists have a term that suggests a useful analogy for such deep architectural commitments.
:149. A body plan is a basic organization established early in the history of an animal lineage and reused in its descendants.
:150. Whales, bats, horses, and humans have transformed homologous forelimb bones for very different functions while retaining the tetrapod pattern they inherited.
:151. Changes to deeply integrated developmental systems can affect many traits at once, so viable variation is often easier within the inherited organization than across it.
:152. This does not mean that evolution literally chose a blueprint or that every feature of a lineage was fixed at its origin.
:153. Body plans themselves evolved, developmental systems retain flexibility, and the comparison should not be pushed beyond its explanatory purpose.
:154. The useful parallel lies in dependency.
:155. A design adopted while a surrounding system is small permits enormous variation above it, while change at the shared foundation becomes costly as more components assume the old arrangement.
:156. The analogy then breaks at a crucial point.
:157. An animal lineage cannot inspect its own developmental history, publish an alternative, or coordinate a planned migration.
:158. A machine-learning architecture is written down, discussed openly, and implemented by people capable of changing it.
:159. No physical law prevents a laboratory from building something different.
:160. The weight holding an incumbent in place consists of compatible tools, sunk investments, acquired skills, organizational routines, performance records, and expectations about what others will support.
:161. Within real budgets and institutions, that weight can be highly binding, but it is not equivalent to biological irreversibility.
:162. People can fund parallel lines of research, design translation layers, preserve data in portable forms, or replace a system gradually.
:163. New hardware, new applications, or a serious failure of the incumbent can also alter the relative costs.
:164. Lock-in is therefore a matter of degree, not a sentence of permanence.
:165. None of this amounts to a claim that the transformer is bad.
:166. It has proved capable of far more than the original machine-translation experiments demonstrated.
:167. The argument would still matter if the transformer were the best general architecture currently available, because present merit and future evolvability are different properties.
:168. Nor does the argument establish that a neglected alternative would have produced better systems.
:169. It establishes that dominance alone cannot settle either question.
:170. Knowing how good an architecture is in isolation would require sustained comparisons among designs given enough support to mature.
:171. Such comparisons are expensive, and not every speculative approach deserves equal investment.
:172. Yet options that are allowed to lose their tools, practitioners, and evaluative vocabulary cannot be summoned instantly when conditions change.
:173. The possibility space may still contain them in theory while no practical path reaches them at acceptable cost.
:174. Successful architectures thicken their surrounding apparatus fastest precisely when changing course seems least urgent.
:175. That process can close some routes, but it can also create modules, skills, and computational capacity from which unexpected routes later open.
:176. Path dependence is not only a story of foreclosure.
:177. It is also a story of how one working solution builds the platform from which the next departure must begin.
:178. Eight authors in 2017 presented a faster and more parallelizable approach to machine translation.
:179. They did not alone choose the blueprint for a generation of technology, because that blueprint accumulated through thousands of later experiments, investments, adaptations, and reasonable decisions.
:180. The boxes in the famous diagram show the computation, but the dependencies that now give the diagram its weight lie mostly outside the frame.
